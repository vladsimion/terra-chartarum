# Terra Chartarum editorial style guide

Status: Final — approved for release use under KAN-276.

Owner: Editorial

Applies to: native essays, substantial revisions to legacy wrappers, room ledes
and interpretive catalogue copy

## Editorial promise

Terra Chartarum reads maps as made arguments. An essay should help a curious
reader see a specific cartographic operation—measurement, selection,
orientation, naming, circulation, classification, omission—without turning
every map into proof of one total theory.

The publication is scholarly but not ceremonial. It makes evidence boundaries
plain, defines specialist terms when they begin useful work, and prefers a
precise qualification to a grand but unsupported claim. Interactive elements
are part of the argument, never a reward placed between sections.

## Voice — KAN-270

### The publication speaks as a close reader

Use an attentive first-person plural sparingly: “we can follow the plate
history” or “we do not infer a measured route.” It invites the reader into an
inspection that the page actually supports. Avoid a royal “we” that substitutes
authority for evidence.

Prefer active, accountable subjects:

- “The atlas groups provinces by title and colour.”
- “The survey office joined local observations to a national grid.”
- “This essay treats the route as schematic.”

Avoid constructions in which decisions seem to happen by themselves:

- “The territory was naturally divided.”
- “It was decided that…”
- “The data shows…” when a collector, method or institution can be named.

### Write from the artifact outward

Begin interpretive passages with something a reader can inspect: a line,
orientation, legend, crop, sequence, catalogue field, plate state or recorded
absence. Move from that observation to historical context and then to the
larger argument. Do not begin with a universal claim and recruit the map as
decoration.

Distinguish three levels:

1. **Visible or recorded:** what the artifact or source contains.
2. **Supported interpretation:** what cited context lets the essay infer.
3. **Boundary:** what the evidence does not establish.

Use confidence in proportion to the level. “The sheet labels…” can be direct.
“The classification supports…” needs evidence. “Every viewer understood…”
almost always exceeds the record.

### Keep the native vocabulary

Define an essay's own terms and use them consistently. The six-dimension
meta-lens is additive discovery metadata; it must not flatten a native
historical vocabulary into portal jargon. Translate a specialist term once,
then retain it when its distinction matters.

## Structure — KAN-271

### Recommended essay movement

The default structure is a reasoning sequence, not a fixed number of headings:

1. **Contract:** name the map set, period, question and thesis.
2. **First inspection:** let one artifact make the problem visible.
3. **Complication:** introduce a second case or scale that resists the easy
   reading.
4. **Mechanism:** explain how the cartographic operation works.
5. **Consequence:** show what becomes possible, powerful or silent.
6. **Boundary:** state what the comparison cannot prove.
7. **Return:** revisit the opening artifact with a sharper question.

Writers may combine or reorder these moves. Every major section still needs one
clear job. If a section cannot be described with an active verb—compare,
distinguish, trace, test, qualify—it is probably a container rather than a step.

### Opening

Open with the reader's likely assumption, an observable feature, or a bounded
problem. Supply scope early: named corpus, date range and geographic or
institutional boundary. State the thesis before the first long historical
detour.

Do not open with:

- a dictionary definition that the essay immediately abandons;
- an imagined universal viewer;
- “Since the dawn of time” chronology;
- a cinematic scene whose details are not sourced;
- a claim that all maps lie, distort or exercise power without specifying how.

### Section and paragraph flow

Frontmatter section IDs and rendered `Section` anchors must match. A section
heading should make a claim or pose the question answered below it.

Paragraphs should ordinarily do one of four things:

- observe evidence;
- explain context or mechanism;
- qualify an inference;
- connect the result to the next question.

When a paragraph tries to do all four, split it. When several short paragraphs
repeat the same role, combine them.

### Conclusion

Return to the opening object or question. State what the reader can now inspect
differently. Do not introduce a new corpus, moral crisis or technology in the
final paragraph. A reading trail may extend inquiry after the argument has
closed.

## Transitions — KAN-272

Transitions should expose the relation between ideas rather than announce page
movement.

### Between paragraphs

Use the first sentence to name the relation:

- **contrast:** “The cadastral parcel makes land discrete; the route refuses
  that surface.”
- **scale:** “At harbour scale the enclave is visible. At regional scale it
  disappears.”
- **evidence:** “The plate supports a claim about revision, not about every
  reader's response.”
- **cause or condition:** “That comparison becomes possible only after the two
  surveys share a reference grid.”
- **return:** “This brings the problem back to the title's apparently stable
  territory.”

Avoid empty bridges such as “It is important to note,” “Moving on,” or “As
mentioned above.” If the relation cannot be named, the order may be wrong.

### Between sections

End a section with the pressure that makes the next one necessary. A strong
handoff can:

- reveal an exception;
- change scale;
- expose a hidden input;
- separate two terms previously treated as one;
- ask what the demonstrated operation costs.

The next heading should answer that pressure. Do not repeat the previous
section's conclusion as a new introduction.

### Into and out of interactives

Before an interactive, state the question and the permitted action: compare,
filter, rotate, scrub or inspect. After it, interpret one or two findings in
prose. A reader who cannot operate the control must receive the complete
argument.

Never write “play with the map below.” Name what changes and what remains fixed.
If scans are not registered for measurement, say so before the slider.

## Tone — KAN-273

The tone is exact, curious and calmly critical.

### Exact

Name the map, maker or institution when known. Use dates as evidence ranges,
not atmosphere. Prefer “the map omits the route from this edition” to “the map
erases history” unless the stronger verb is supported by a documented process
of removal.

### Curious

Let difficult objects remain interesting. Avoid writing as if historical
mapmakers existed only to fail modern tests. Ask what work an unfamiliar
orientation, scale or classification performed in its own setting.

### Calmly critical

Power and silence are central, but intensity comes from evidence rather than
accusatory adjectives. Explain the mechanism by which a survey, boundary,
catalogue or interface distributes authority. Avoid “obviously,” “simply,”
“merely,” “primitive,” “backward” and “irrational” unless directly analysing a
historical quotation.

### Formality and address

Use clear contemporary English with contractions only where they prevent
stiffness. Address the reader through invitations to inspect—“notice,”
“compare,” “follow”—not presumed emotions. Do not claim that an image is
shocking, beautiful or confusing for everyone.

Humour may clarify a convention or puncture false neutrality, but it must not
target historical subjects, affected communities or a reader's knowledge.

## Pacing — KAN-274

Pacing is the management of attention, not a target sentence length.

### Establish a rhythm

Alternate among:

- close inspection of an object;
- contextual explanation;
- comparison or counterexample;
- a short statement of consequence.

Three dense contextual sections in succession will bury the map. Three
interactives in succession will fragment the thesis. Return to visible evidence
after an abstract passage.

### Slow down at interpretive risk

Give more space when:

- a term changes meaning across periods;
- a visual difference may result from crop, projection or scan quality;
- a category affects living or historically marginalised people;
- a causal claim depends on several institutions;
- uncertainty or disagreement changes the conclusion.

Use explicit boundaries and source notes. Do not accelerate through contested
evidence with a list of citations.

### Move quickly through scaffolding

Compress:

- biographical context that does not affect the map;
- chronology already available in a timeline;
- repeated explanations of the same interface;
- cataloguing detail that belongs in the collection record;
- throat-clearing before a section's claim.

### Place interactives at turns

An interactive belongs where the reader must perform the essay's central
distinction: before/after, route/territory, state/edition, centre/orientation,
record/journey. Let the prose prepare the terms; let the interaction make the
difference inspectable; let the following paragraph name the result.

### Length and density

Prefer section length determined by argumentative work. For the current
publication, most major sections land between roughly 250 and 700 words, but
evidence decides. A very short section should create a deliberate turn. A long
section needs internal signposts or a second heading.

Reading-time metadata is an estimate, not a quota. Cut repetition before
cutting the qualification that keeps a claim honest.

## Non-negotiable boundaries

- No invented quotations, page references, provenance or viewer reactions.
- No unsourced precision disguised by an interactive.
- No historical boundary shown as a present, natural fact without date and
  authority.
- No use of colour as the sole carrier of meaning.
- No essential claims about cultures, nations, religions or mobile peoples.
- No publication-stage manifest without approved outline and narrative review.
- No broken section anchors or cross-links.

## Worked cohesion examples — KAN-275

### Observation before interpretation

Weak:

> Mercator's imperial map intentionally makes Europe dominant.

Why it fails: the projection property is real, but the sentence assigns a
specific intention without evidence and collapses projection, use and later
reception.

Preferred:

> At world scale, Mercator enlarges high latitudes. That property makes the
> projection unsuitable for comparing continental area, regardless of the
> motive of a particular publisher.

The revision starts with a measurable property, connects it to a bounded use
and leaves intention for evidence that can support it.

### Transition as reasoning

Weak:

> The atlas also contains city maps. Moving on, we will consider borders.

Preferred:

> The city symbols organise the province, but they do not explain where its
> authority stops. The border supplies that second administrative claim.

The revision names why the next section is necessary.

### Evidence boundary near the claim

Weak:

> The route proves that Venetian merchants regularly stopped at every port.

Preferred:

> The ordered waypoints define the route sequence used by this reconstruction.
> Until the Lane chronology receives page-level verification, they do not
> establish one immutable itinerary or a call at every port in every year.

The qualification is not buried in a final methodology note.

### Interactive with an argumentative job

Weak:

> Use the slider below to explore the maps.

Preferred:

> Drag between the two states to inspect the recut South American coastline.
> The scans differ in crop and cannot support pixel measurement; the comparison
> is limited to the documented engraved change.

The preferred introduction names action, evidence and constraint.

### Conclusion that returns

Weak:

> Maps remain powerful today, and future technologies will create new
> challenges.

Preferred:

> The outline that opened the essay now reads less like a country's natural
> body and more like a rehearsed administrative claim. The next time it appears
> without neighbours, ask which institutions taught the line to stand alone.

The revision closes the established argument instead of opening a new one.

## Quick-reference review checklist — KAN-275

### Argument and evidence

- [ ] The thesis names a cartographic operation, corpus and boundary.
- [ ] Each major claim begins from inspectable or cited evidence.
- [ ] Observation, interpretation and uncertainty are distinguishable.
- [ ] Dates, quotations, page references and provenance are verified.
- [ ] No category, border or orientation is described as natural by default.

### Cohesion

- [ ] The opening states the essay's promise before extended context.
- [ ] Every section has one argumentative job and advances the thesis.
- [ ] Transitions name contrast, scale, evidence, cause or return.
- [ ] Pacing slows at contested evidence and compresses repeated scaffolding.
- [ ] The conclusion returns to the opening object or question.

### Interactives and access

- [ ] Every interactive answers a question prepared by the prose.
- [ ] A complete text interpretation exists without operating the control.
- [ ] Measurement, registration and uncertainty limits are stated nearby.
- [ ] Keyboard, reduced-motion and non-colour meanings are preserved.
- [ ] Section anchors and local cross-links resolve.

### Release

- [ ] Outline decision is `approved` or `approved-with-notes`.
- [ ] Narrative decision is `approved` or `approved-with-notes`.
- [ ] Review date and notes path exist in the manifest.
- [ ] `npm run editorial:validate` passes.
- [ ] `npm run build` passes with the editorial gate enabled.
- [ ] No hold criterion in `data/editorial/release-policy.json` applies.
