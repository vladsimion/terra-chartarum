# Invisible Maps of Religion — research dossier

**Ticket:** KAN-124

## Scope

This is a case study of medieval European and Byzantine Christian map forms,
approximately 570–1581. It does not propose a universal theory of “religious
maps,” does not treat Christianity as a proxy for religion, and does not infer a
single medieval viewer. The argument is narrower: selected maps make sacred
orientation operational through three visible grammars — centre, direction and
route.

## Method

The essay describes what the selected artifacts make prominent. “Centre” means a
graphic or compositional centre; “orientation” names the direction placed at the
top or the direction toward which a route tends; “route” means an ordered sequence
of stations. These fields can overlap. Hereford centres Jerusalem while orienting
east upward; Matthew Paris privileges an itinerary without pretending to preserve
uniform scale. The interactive therefore filters by grammar rather than assigning
each object to one exclusive type.

The corpus includes facsimiles and digital reproductions. Colour, crop, exposure
and material condition are never compared as if they were stable historical data.
Claims about contents come from custodial records and specialist scholarship; the
site’s images demonstrate arrangement, not microscopic codicology.
