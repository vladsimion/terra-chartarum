## What you are looking at

Four ports: Jaffa, where a pilgrim bound for Jerusalem actually landed; Acre,
which the kingdom of Jerusalem was administered from for most of the thirteenth
century; Alexandria, the Egyptian trade the fourteenth-century recovery
treatises proposed to cut; and Famagusta, where the eastern traffic went after 1291.

## How to read this layer

This layer is four records out of ten. The dataset behind it also holds
Jerusalem at the centre of two world images, the destination an itinerary points
at without drawing, a Holy Land described in divisions taken from Acre, that
land ruled into squares for an expedition nobody mounted, and a printed clover
leaf from 1581 that centres Jerusalem anyway.

None of those six is here, and their absence is the argument rather than a gap.
The middle of a mappa mundi is not at 31.78° N. Pinning it there would convert a
claim about what the world is arranged around into a claim about where a city
is, which is exactly the substitution the essay is about.

## Historical scope

1099 to 1372 - from the capture of Jerusalem to the century after Acre fell. The
register deliberately outlives the kingdom, because the network did.

## Sources and evidence

Burchard of Mount Sion's description of the Holy Land and Marino Sanudo
Torsello's recovery treatise, both read here through nineteenth- and
seventeenth-century printed editions that are out of copyright. Neither has been
read at the page: every locator reads `pending`, and the two records resting on
Sanudo carry low confidence for that reason.

## Reconstruction and uncertainty

Positions are the modern towns', as everywhere in this corpus. The Arabic name
forms on the place records are romanisations taken from published reference
works rather than readings of an Arabic source, and that is recorded as an open
item rather than left as an impression of thoroughness.

## Editorial decisions

Acre and Alexandria point at Venetian Maritime Network layers rather than
re-authoring what those layers already publish with their own evidence.

The 1581 clover leaf is in the dataset and carries no source record at all. An
early-modern woodcut is a witness to the sixteenth century; letting it into the
source corpus would quietly make it a witness to the twelfth.
