## What you are looking at

The region of the southern hemisphere within which European maps drew a
continent, for roughly two and a half centuries before anyone saw one.

It is a latitude envelope. It is not a coastline, and it is not any particular
map's coastline.

## How to read this layer

The outline is dashed, which in this family always means Terra Chartarum drew it
rather than a source. It may not be measured, and no point on it corresponds to
anything an engraver put on a plate.

The layer is drawn as an outline rather than a filled area on purpose. Filling it
would put a continent on the map, and the argument of this whole programme is
that there was no continent to put there yet.

## Historical scope

1531 to 1775. The lower bound is the generation of printed world maps that
carried the southern continent widely; the upper is the voyage that bounded it.

## Sources and evidence

The evidence class is `conjectured`: land drawn because reasoning and inherited
authority implied it should exist, not because anyone reported it.

No map in the object register has been examined. Four candidates are recorded -
Fine, Mercator, Ortelius and a seventeenth-century comparator - and for none of
them has a holding copy or a plate state been identified. Until that happens
there is nothing to digitise, which is why this layer holds an envelope.

## Reconstruction and uncertainty

Confidence is low and the review state is raw. The honest reading of this layer
is: _maps put a continent somewhere in here_. Anything more precise would be
Terra Chartarum's invention rather than the period's.

## Editorial decisions

Drawing a plausible southern coastline would have been easy and would have looked
better. It would also have manufactured exactly the false confidence the essay
exists to examine, and a reader would have had no way to tell our line from an
engraver's. The envelope is deliberately too crude to be mistaken for evidence.
