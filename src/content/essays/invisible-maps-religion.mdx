# Invisible Maps of Religion

## Sacred space before the border

The modern political map makes a persuasive promise: every place has a position,
every boundary has a side, and every journey can be measured against a continuous
surface. The promise is so familiar that maps which organise space differently are
often introduced as imperfect steps toward it. East appears at the top and must be
“corrected” in the caption. Jerusalem occupies the centre and is treated as a
symbolic intrusion into geography. A road expands to fit a sequence of towns and is
described as distorted because its intervals cannot be read as uniform distance.

Those descriptions identify real differences, but they misunderstand the work.

A sacred centre is not an error in the location of a city. It is an instruction
about how the world is to be approached. An east-up image is not a compass failure.
It establishes a direction from which creation, paradise or salvation can be read.
An itinerary whose stations swell and contract is not a damaged scale bar. It makes
the ordered passage from one place to another more important than the empty ground
between them.

Religious maps are “invisible” here in two senses. First, many spatial orders that
shape ritual life do not require a map sheet at all. A body turns toward an altar.
A
pilgrim follows a remembered sequence. A calendar joins a day to a place. A city is
approached as a constellation of shrines, gates and stories. These are maps enacted
as posture, repetition and route.

Second, when such orders do appear on parchment, mosaic or paper, a later geographic
vocabulary can make their actual operation disappear. We notice coastlines but not
the reading path. We ask whether north is up before asking what the top means. We
measure the margin between cities while overlooking the fact that the image has
placed sacred history in the same field as geography.

This essay examines nine artifacts made between the sixth and sixteenth centuries:
the Madaba mosaic, the Saint-Sever Beatus world map, the lost Ebstorf map as known
through facsimile, the Psalter map, Matthew Paris’s itinerary toward Jerusalem, the
Hereford Mappa Mundi, a 1472 printed T–O diagram, the world map in the 1475
_Rudimentum Novitiorum_, and Heinrich Bünting’s clover-leaf world. They do not form a
single tradition, still less a universal category called “the religious map.” They
belong to different media, institutions, uses and centuries.

The comparison is therefore disciplined by three modest questions. What does the
artifact place at or near its compositional centre? Which direction does it make
prominent? Does it organise movement as a sequence of stations? The answers overlap.
They do not turn nine maps into nine specimens of one type. Instead they reveal
three graphic grammars through which sacred space could become visible, memorable
and usable before the bordered, north-up, metrically continuous map became our
default expectation.

<Section id="centre-is-instruction" title="A centre is an instruction" rooms={['theatre', 'city']}>

A centre is often mistaken for a claim about coordinates. On a measured map, the
centre may be nothing more than the consequence of a chosen extent: the point left
over after a rectangle has been fitted around a territory. But a compositional centre
can also be a verb. It centres attention. It establishes hierarchy. It turns the
surrounding field into a set of relations to one privileged place.

The Madaba mosaic makes the operation unusually concrete. Laid into the floor of a
Byzantine church in the sixth century, the surviving map represents part of the Holy
Land and neighbouring regions. Jerusalem is enlarged and internally differentiated.
Streets, gates and buildings occupy more visual space than a uniform scale would
grant them. The city is not simply plotted. It is made available as a destination
whose sacred and urban density deserves inspection.

The Hereford Mappa Mundi works at another scale. Hereford Cathedral’s collection
guide describes a geographic circle fifty-two inches across, centred on Jerusalem
and crowded with roughly 420 cities and towns. Biblical episodes, animals, peoples,
classical legends and marvels inhabit the same skin. The centre does not erase the
rest of the world. It supplies the hinge by which a great accumulation becomes a
Christian history of the inhabited earth.

Bünting’s 1581 clover leaf pushes centring toward emblem. Europe, Asia and Africa
become three leaves arranged around Jerusalem. The graphic is immediately legible
as something other than a navigational world map. Yet it is not spatially empty.
Continents, cities, seas and directions are recruited into a memorable Christian
figure. Jerusalem holds the leaves together, less like a point in a coordinate
system than the pin of a devotional diagram.

These centres do not mean the same thing. Madaba’s Jerusalem is a monumental city
within a regional mosaic; Hereford’s is the centre of an encyclopedic world; Bünting’s
is the joint of a printed emblem. “Sacred centre” is valuable only if it preserves
those differences.

The answer matters beyond the history of cartography. Centring is one way
institutions make values feel like space. A court places the ruler at the point from
which rank radiates. A city plan enlarges the civic monuments through which the city
wants to be known. A transit diagram centres interchange rather than land area.
Sacred maps make the operation explicit enough that we can recognise it elsewhere.
Their lesson is not that belief contaminates an otherwise neutral geography. It is
that every centre deserves to be read as a choice.

</Section>

<Section id="east-is-up" title="When east is up" rooms={['theatre', 'map']}>

Orientation is now so closely associated with north that the word can conceal its
own history. To orient originally carries the east within it: the _oriens_, the place
of rising. On many medieval Christian world maps, placing east at the top joined the
page to a cosmological and scriptural direction. Paradise could occupy the upper
edge; the inhabited world unfolded below.

The Saint-Sever Beatus map, copied in the eleventh century within an Apocalypse
commentary, is not merely a geographic insert awaiting modern correction. Its form
belongs to a manuscript argument. Lands, seas, apostles and histories participate
in an account of the world through which Christian teaching travelled. The top of
the page does not behave like the top of a road atlas. It is part of a reading order.

The Ebstorf map, probably made in the thirteenth century and destroyed in 1943,
survives through photographs and facsimiles. Its world was figured in relation to
the body of Christ: head above, hands at the sides, feet below. The device makes
orientation bodily. The world is not placed beside theology as an illustration. It
is held within a sacred figure whose anatomy supplies the page with direction.

The small Psalter map compresses an immense world into a devotional book. Its size
is important. Monumental and portable mappaemundi cannot have demanded identical
viewing practices. A large display receives moving viewers; a manuscript image
meets a reader at hand. Yet both can use orientation to connect the geography of the
page to the moral and sacred order of a larger text.

To call these images “east-up” is accurate but incomplete. The phrase still begins
from north-up and marks a deviation. A stronger description asks what the top does.
It may locate paradise, stage Christ, begin a history, or simply inherit a
convention whose particular force changed in a new context. The evidence is the
arrangement. The intention and response require more caution.

That caution prevents a familiar romantic error. Medieval viewers were not
incapable of practical direction, distance or worldly geography. Nautical charts,
estate maps, local surveys and itineraries existed beside world diagrams. The same
culture could sustain more than one spatial grammar because the grammars answered
different questions. A portolan chart asked how a coastline could be crossed by
bearings. A mappamundi could ask how places, peoples and histories belonged within
an ordered creation.

North-up political maps also answer a question, even when their convention feels
natural. They ask how territories can be placed on a continuous, repeatable surface.
Their orientation is powerful partly because it no longer has to announce itself.
East-up maps restore the visibility of orientation as a choice.

</Section>

<Section id="world-as-history" title="The world as simultaneous history" rooms={['theatre', 'archive']}>

The most difficult feature of a mappamundi may not be its outline or orientation.
It is the presence of different times in one spatial field.

On the Hereford map, biblical events, classical stories, contemporary settlements,
distant peoples and natural marvels do not queue in chronological panels. They are
distributed through geography. The reader moves across places and encounters a
history that is simultaneously available to the eye. The world becomes an archive
whose documents are locations.

“Simultaneous” must be used carefully. It describes the surface of the artifact, not
a claim that medieval people could not distinguish past from present. Manuscripts,
chronicles and liturgies supplied elaborate chronological orders. The point is that
a map can make time navigable through adjacency. A journey of the eye joins episodes
that a chronological list would separate.

David Woodward’s survey of medieval mappaemundi resists treating the artifacts as
one stable cartographic class. Diagrams, zonal schemes, T–O forms and richly
illustrated world maps differ radically in scale and content. Evelyn Edson’s account
of mapping time and space likewise helps us see that geographic and historical
organisation were not competing layers waiting to be separated by modernity. Their
combination could be the point.

The timeline below marks the dates of the nine artifacts, not the dates of every
event they picture. That distinction is the exhibit. A thirteenth-century map can
contain Eden, apostolic travel, ancient cities and contemporary knowledge without
becoming a thirteenth-century snapshot. The artifact date locates the act of
assembly; the represented histories exceed it in both directions.

This spatial archive can authorise as well as remember. Selecting which histories
belong to which places is an act of power. Peoples at the edge may be made
marvellous, dangerous or incomplete. Jerusalem’s centre can organise a Christian
world while placing other traditions inside that organisation. A sacred geography
does not become harmless because it is non-metric.

The map’s density can also fool a present viewer into celebrating “wonder” while
ignoring classification. Strange peoples and creatures are not neutral ornaments.
They participate in a moral geography that distinguishes centre from margin,
familiar from foreign, orthodox from other. To read the map generously is not to
withhold critique. It is to criticise the operation the artifact actually performs,
rather than accusing it of failing to perform ours.

</Section>

<Section id="road-becomes-devotion" title="The road becomes devotion" rooms={['road', 'city']}>

If a centred world map organises relations around a privileged place, an itinerary
organises them through succession. Matthew Paris’s prefatory material in Royal MS 14
C VII includes a route from London toward the Holy Land. Cities appear as stations
in a journey whose order matters more than uniform intervals.

The itinerary can be read with the finger. One place leads to the next. Rivers,
gates, seas and settlements become decisions in a chain. A traveller does not need
to possess the world at once; the route makes a limited world sufficient for
movement.

This is an invisible map in the most practical sense. Much pilgrimage knowledge
could live in spoken instruction, hospitality networks, remembered landmarks and
ritual repetition. The manuscript does not invent the route. It gives selected
relations a durable visual sequence. The road becomes a devotional structure
because reaching the next station advances both travel and sacred purpose.

Compare that sequential logic with Hereford’s centred plenitude. The slider is not
an overlay of matching geographies, and its differently proportioned reproductions
must not be measured against one another. It is a comparison of reading behaviour.
On one side, the eye can roam outward and inward across a world gathered around
Jerusalem. On the other, it descends through a vertical chain in which order carries
the journey.

The distinction between centre and route is not absolute. Pilgrimage routes tend
toward sacred centres. A centred map can contain roads and journeys. The value of
the two grammars is analytical: they let us notice whether an image asks first for
relation to a privileged point or for progress through ordered stations.

Madaba joins both. Jerusalem is an enlarged destination, but the mosaic also maps a
regional field through which the city can be approached. City and road are not
separate rooms in lived experience. The route produces the city as a sacred centre,
and the centre gives the route its direction.

That feedback survives in modern systems. A transit map makes the interchange
larger by multiplying connections. A tourist map enlarges the monument because
paths converge there. A digital route collapses intermediate space until the next
turn approaches. These are not religious maps, but they demonstrate how sequence
and centre can organise use without respecting uniform geographic scale.

The medieval itinerary therefore deserves more than the forgiving label
“schematic.” It records a theory of relevance. What the traveller needs next is
given space. What can remain between instructions is compressed. The route is not
missing a complete territory; it refuses to make complete territory the condition
of movement.

</Section>

<Section id="print-remembers" title="Print remembers the diagram" rooms={['archive', 'theatre']}>

Printing did not suddenly replace sacred diagrams with measured geography. Early
printed books carried compact world schemes into new material and editorial
systems.

The T–O diagram in the first printed edition of Isidore’s _Etymologiae_ in 1472 is
small enough to be mistaken for a sign rather than a map. A circle and internal
divisions partition the inhabited world. Its power lies in compression. The diagram
can be repeated, attached to text and remembered. It is a world made portable by
reducing spatial abundance to an ordered figure.

The 1475 _Rudimentum Novitiorum_, printed by Lucas Brandis in Lübeck, gives the
relationship between book, history and map more room. The Library of Congress
catalogue describes a substantial incunable devoted to world history, with
illustrations, genealogical tables and two maps. Here mapping participates in an
apparatus for organising universal history. The printed map is not an autonomous
window slipped accidentally into the volume. It belongs to the book’s machinery of
sequence and reference.

A century later, Bünting’s clover leaf demonstrates that emblematic sacred
geography remained productive within print culture. The image is witty, memorable
and geographically referential at once. Its three leaves transform continental
division into a Christian sign centred on Jerusalem. Printing extends the diagram’s
circulation, but the diagram also gives print a compact object worth circulating.

It is tempting to narrate these objects as survivors. On that account, schematic
religious geography lingers while “real” cartography advances through Ptolemaic
coordinates, navigation, survey and atlas publishing. The language of survival
already decides the contest. It treats continued use as residue rather than
evidence that different images remained useful for different intellectual work.

The same decades could support portolan charts, regional maps, city views, classical
geographies, itineraries and sacred world diagrams. Print increased the possibilities
for juxtaposition. It did not force every map to answer one question.

The archive can disguise this plurality by sorting objects into later categories.
A small T–O diagram becomes “symbolic”; a portolan becomes “practical”; a
mappamundi becomes “medieval”; an atlas becomes “modern.” The labels help retrieval
but can harden into a story of replacement. The catalogue records linked throughout
this essay keep medium, holding, rights, relationship and essay context together so
that one formal feature does not consume the object.

Print remembers the diagram because memory is part of diagrammatic power. A form
that can be recognised at small scale, repeated across copies and recalled after the
page is closed has achieved something different from topographic completeness. Its
economy is not emptiness. It is a technology of retention.

</Section>

<Section id="boundary-of-this-argument" title="The boundary of this argument" rooms={['theatre', 'road', 'city']}>

The title “Invisible Maps of Religion” risks promising too much. Religion is not a
single spatial system, and Christian artifacts from medieval Europe and Byzantium
cannot stand for sacred mapping across Jewish, Islamic, Hindu, Buddhist, Indigenous
or other traditions. Even within Christian practice, nine surviving and digitised
objects cannot represent the spatial knowledge of every community, traveller or
reader.

This essay therefore ends where its research began: with a boundary around the
claim.

The corpus demonstrates that selected Christian maps between about 570 and 1581 use
centre, orientation and route to organise sacred space. It shows that those grammars
can overlap, that geography and history can share a surface, and that early print
can extend schematic traditions rather than merely supersede them. It does not
prove that every feature was read identically, or that non-metric maps were free of
politics, or that a single Christian worldview existed unchanged for a millennium.

The boundary is not an apology. It is a method for keeping comparison honest.
Without it, an attractive thesis about sacred geography would turn difference into
decoration. With it, the three grammars become questions that can travel without
prejudging the answers.

Where is attention centred, and by whom?

Which direction orders the field, and what does the top or front make authoritative?

What sequence of stations turns movement into a meaningful route?

Those questions can now be taken into other rooms and traditions. The{' '}

<a href="/rooms/road/">Road</a> asks how journeys become knowledge. The
<a href="/rooms/city/">City</a> asks how monuments, streets and memories produce an urban whole. The
<a href="/rooms/theatre/">Theatre</a> asks how a map stages a world for inspection. The room system
does not erase the boundary of this case study; it makes the boundary visible while opening paths
beyond it.

The final lesson is simple. A map need not draw a border to organise territory. It
can turn the body, privilege a centre, order a journey, join a story to a place, or
make a diagram memorable enough to guide action after the object itself is gone.

Sacred space is not invisible because it lacks form. It becomes invisible when we
look only for the forms our own maps have taught us to expect.

</Section>
