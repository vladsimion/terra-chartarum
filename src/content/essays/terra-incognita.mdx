# Terra Incognita

## Antarctica existed on maps for two and a half centuries before anyone saw it.

That sentence is the whole essay. What follows is not the story of who reached
the ice first. It is the story of what a map does when there is nothing to look
at: how it fills the space anyway, how the filling acquires authority, and how
hard it turns out to be to take back.

Discovery, on this account, is not the moment an empty space is filled. It is the
much longer process by which a full space is emptied and then, slowly and
differently, filled again from evidence.

<Section id="necessitas" title="The world requires a south" rooms={['theatre', 'earth']}>

Before Antarctica was seen it was deduced.

The reasoning was old and it was not stupid. A globe with continents crowded into
the north seemed to want something in the south, and a tradition running back
through late-medieval cosmography supplied it. By the time the printing press
began turning out world maps in quantity, the southern continent was already
there to be printed: large, temperate, and drawn with a coastline.

The temptation for a modern reader is to say that cartographers _believed_ in it.
That is a stronger claim than the evidence supports, and it is one this project
declines to make. What can be said is that the argument existed, that the
coastline was printed, and that the two are different things. A map is not a
diary.

What the Atlas shows for this act is deliberately crude: an envelope, not a coast.
No map in the register has been examined closely enough to digitise, and drawing
a plausible southern shoreline would have produced exactly the false confidence
this section is about.

</Section>

<Section id="auctoritas" title="Drawing what has not been seen" rooms={['map', 'archive']}>

How does a guess become a fact?

By being copied. Engravers worked from other engravers' plates; a publisher who
wanted a world map did not commission a new survey of the southern ocean, because
there was none to be had. The southern coast passed from plate to plate,
acquiring detail on the way, and agreement between maps came to look like
corroboration when it was really inheritance.

The engraving conventions of the period made this worse in a way nobody intended.
A burin cuts one kind of line. There was no visual grammar for _this coast is
reported_ as against _this coast was surveyed_, so both arrived on the page at
the same confidence. A reader could not tell, and neither, after a generation or
two, could the mapmaker.

This is the grammar the rest of the essay uses, and it is the grammar the data
model enforces: **conjectured** land is drawn because reasoning implied it,
**reported** land because someone said so, **observed** land because someone saw
it, and **reconciled** land because someone fitted several observations
together. Collapsing those into one category is the error the whole programme is
built to avoid.

</Section>

<Section id="orbis" title="Coronelli at the edge of the known world" rooms={['theatre', 'map']}>

Vincenzo Maria Coronelli made globes. That is not an incidental biographical fact:
it is a constraint, and it is why he belongs in this argument.

A sheet map can leave a corner vague. A globe cannot leave a hole. Whatever the
sources permitted, the southern calotte had to be engraved with something, and
what a comprehensive cosmographer put there under that pressure is a better
question than what he privately thought.

The object is no longer unseen. _Terre Artiche_, David Rumsey 12186.101, is
Arctic to the neatline. It sits in a seven-page polar section of the _Atlante
Veneto_ beside a second Arctic plate and no southern one. The asymmetry is real,
but the obvious explanation for it turned out to be wrong.

Coronelli did not neglect the south. On printed page 76 he names Nuyts, Tasman,
New Zealand, Quiros and Carpentaria, then dismisses a succession of other claimed
lands as inventions or fables because nobody could find them again. The missing
southern plate therefore reads less like a gap in attention than a judgement
about what could responsibly be engraved.

That last step is this essay's interpretation, not Coronelli's statement, and it
remains at medium confidence. Three of the seven leaves are still unread, no
southern plate has been ruled out across the other twelve _Atlante Veneto_
volumes, the globe's southern calotte has not been examined, and Milanesi's study
of Coronelli's workshop is still unconsulted.

What is secure is narrower and more useful. On page 70 Coronelli asks whether the
four polar islands inherited from Mercator were drawn from life or invented by
the imagination. At the centre of the Arctic plate, a ring of mountains encloses
a legend naming Willoughby, the 1633 Spitsbergen party and Martens, with dates
and latitudes. Graphic and textual evidence distinguish inheritance from report
in his own voice. The act can now be written; it cannot yet be released.

</Section>

<Section id="vacuum" title="The blank that Cook produced" rooms={['map', 'road']}>

Between 1772 and 1775 the _Resolution_ crossed the Antarctic Circle and worked
round the high southern latitudes, keeping a record of where she had been.

She found no continent. The chart published in 1777 shows the tracks, and the
tracks are the argument: wherever they run, the drawn continent could not be.

<CooksBlank caption="Terra Australis withdrawn, not erased. The dotted outline is Terra Chartarum's linework, not any engraver's coastline." />

The overclaim to resist here is the one everyone reaches for. Cook did not prove
there was no land in the far south. Land was sighted within fifty years of his
return, and it is there now. What his voyage destroyed was the _temperate,
habitable, reachable_ Terra Australis that the maps had drawn, and what it
produced in its place was a bounded space: whatever remained had to be smaller,
further south, and colder than anyone had been drawing.

That is the hinge of the essay. The map after Cook shows less land and contains
more knowledge, and the blank is not ignorance. It is a result.

</Section>

<Section id="limen" title="The continent appears" rooms={['border', 'map']}>

Then, within a single southern summer, three parties.

The usual way to write this is to pick one and call them the discoverer. This
essay does not, because the disagreement is not about what happened. It is about
what counts.

A sighting of an ice front is not a sighting of the mainland. A sighting of the
mainland is not a landing. A sealing master's log is a record, but a different
kind of record from a naval survey, and it survives differently. Each of the 1820
claims answers a different one of those questions, which is why comparing their
dates settles nothing at all.

There is a fourth party in the dataset with no date and no position: the sealing
voyages that may have seen land earlier and kept no public account. It is an
argument from silence, and this publication's own work on the Dacian _hiatus_ is
about how treacherous those are. It stays in the record as a possibility the
archive cannot settle, and it is there mainly to stop the field looking closed.

The two programmes are the same problem from opposite ends. <EssayLink slug="dacia">Terra
Sigillata</EssayLink> works on a territory that was inhabited, administered and
walked for centuries and left almost no map; this essay works on maps of a
territory nobody had seen. In one the ground is certain and the record is
missing. In the other the record is abundant and the ground is missing. Both
argue that the blank on a map is evidence about the map, and that reading it as
evidence about the world is the error the discipline keeps repeating.

What follows over the next twenty years is not discovery in the singular but
accumulation: Weddell into an unusually open sea, Biscoe and Balleny working for
a commercial house, then in one crowded season the American, French and British
squadrons charting overlapping coast and disagreeing about it.

Nineteen of those positions are in the Atlas, each carrying how it was arrived
at rather than only where it was. None has been read against its source, and the
layer says so on its own record.

<Section id="error" title="Lands that vanished" rooms={['archive', 'map']}>

Some of what was charted was not there.

The treatment of this in popular accounts is ridicule, and ridicule is useless.
The most instructive vanished feature in this corpus was reported by James Clark
Ross, who was the most careful observer of the period, working in air where
distance and elevation are famously hard to judge. If a mistake like that tells
us anything, it is about seeing, not about him.

So a disproved feature here keeps four things: who introduced it, what was
actually reported, why that was a reasonable thing to record at the time, and
what later evidence said. A record that keeps only the last of those is not
history; it is a scorecard.

Two further disciplines apply. Saying a feature was later removed from the charts
is a record of non-confirmation. Saying it was a mirage, or an ice island misread
as land, is a much stronger claim that needs its own source, and no feature in
this corpus currently has one. And a feature reported as removed but with no
authority for the removal is filed as _unresolved_, not _disproved_, because a
disproof that cannot name what disproved it is not a disproof.

The vanished coast that reaches furthest into this essay is one reported in the
Weddell Sea in 1823, in the same extraordinary ice year that let Weddell sail
further south than anyone would manage again for a lifetime. Ninety years later a
ship drifted through that water. She is the subject of the last act.

</Section>

<Section id="forma" title="From fragments to a continent" rooms={['archive', 'map']}>

By 1910 the Admiralty could print a single sheet compiling ice observations from
Cook in 1772 through to Shackleton in 1909: eleven voyages, one hundred and
thirty-seven years, one chart.

It is tempting to read that as the moment the fragments finally cohere. The
chart's own record undercuts the reading, and this is the essay's best-evidenced
finding rather than its most speculative.

The 1910 sheet is a reissue. An earlier compilation of 1874 carries the same
voyages up to Ross in 1843, and credits something else besides: a set of papers
on icebergs in the southern ocean, an analysis by a man who never went south. The
1910 issue adds Scott and Shackleton, and drops the papers.

So a cumulative chart is not a summary of everything known. It is a dated state
of knowledge that gets revised, including by subtraction, and the archive it
layers is one somebody curated. That is a more interesting claim than
accumulation, and it comes from reading two catalogue titles side by side.

</Section>

<Section id="deriva" title="A geography that would not stay still" rooms={['road', 'theatre']}>

In 1914 Shackleton announced a crossing of the continent: into the Weddell Sea,
ashore, over the pole, and out by the Ross Sea. It is a clean line on a map and
nothing ever travelled along it.

What happened instead is the hardest navigation problem in this essay, and it has
almost nothing to do with courage. On 19 January 1915 the _Endurance_ was beset,
and for the next nine months her position changed continuously while nobody
steered anything. The ship was fixed to a floe. The floe was not fixed to
anything.

<EnduranceDrift caption="Plan, approach, besetment, drift, loss, and the two boat journeys. A dotted line is Terra Chartarum's drawing, not a recorded track." />

Frank Worsley is the figure this act is about. When the ship was crushed and the
party camped on the ice, the question of where they were did not go away; it got
harder, because the answer was changing without them. And when the boats finally
launched, the _James Caird_ had to cross eight hundred miles of the southern
ocean to a small island, with few opportunities to take a sight and no margin at
all for an error in longitude.

The distinction the whole act rests on is between a position that was _observed_
with an instrument and one that was _reckoned_ from course, speed and elapsed
time. Both are historical evidence. They are not the same evidence, and a track
drawn without the difference is a story rather than a record.

</Section>

<Section id="coordinata" title="What a coordinate is" rooms={['theatre', 'archive']}>

The _Endurance_ sank on 21 November 1915, and Worsley recorded a position for it.

That figure has since been recomputed from his logbook by navigation
researchers, working through his sights and his chronometer. The wreck itself was
found in 2022. The obvious thing to do here is to print both coordinates and the
distance between them.

This essay does not, yet, because it has not read either figure in a source it
can cite, and because a distance between two numbers whose uncertainties are
unknown is not a measurement. Secondary reporting on the discovery routinely
confuses the two positions and then quotes a separation between them, which
cannot all be true at once.

What can be said without either number is the thing the whole essay has been
building towards. A coordinate looks like a fact. It is a result: an observation
taken by a person with an instrument, a time taken from a clock that was probably
wrong by some amount nobody could measure, an assumption about the ship's
movement since the last sight, and an arithmetic. Every one of those carries an
error, and the coordinate carries all of them.

A continent was drawn for two and a half centuries because that seemed reasonable.
It was unbuilt by a voyage that found nothing, rebuilt from sightings that
contradicted each other, corrected by removing things that were not there, and
compiled onto a sheet that was itself revised. At the end of it a ship recorded
where she went down, and it took a hundred and seven years and a recomputation of
her navigator's arithmetic to find out how close that was.

The map was never a picture of the world. It was always a record of what somebody
could know.

</Section>
