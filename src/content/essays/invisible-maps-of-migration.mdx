# Invisible Maps of Migration

## The journey and the record

A migration map usually begins after movement has been translated into data.
One place is called an origin, another a destination, and a line joins them.
Width may represent people; colour may distinguish status or period. The result
can reveal scale and direction. It can also erase almost everything that made a
journey a journey: waiting, return, coercion, detour, kinship, money, danger,
paperwork and the decision not to move.

Migration is invisible where it exceeds the administrative event that records
it. A border crossing is not a life. A census residence five years apart does
not contain the path between. A ship manifest names those the system chose and
was able to enumerate. An asylum decision documents a legal encounter, not the
whole geography of flight.

This essay separates movement from its trace. The trace is valuable evidence,
but it must not impersonate the person.

<Section id="flow-line-is-summary" title="A flow line is a summary" rooms={['road', 'map']}>

Charles Joseph Minard's flow maps demonstrated how width could make quantity
spatially legible. Later migration maps inherited the grammar: bands leave
regions, converge on ports, cross oceans and divide at destinations. The view
is powerful because it makes aggregate relation visible.

Every band contains classification. Does it count journeys, people or
households? Is return migration subtracted, shown separately or lost? Are
forced transport, labour recruitment and voluntary relocation combined?
What period has been compressed into the line?

The apparent route may be no route at all. An origin–destination dataset often
lacks intermediate places, so the cartographer draws a curve for readability.
That curve should not be interpreted as a corridor travelled. A schematic
connection needs a legend as explicit as a measured road.

Scale changes meaning. At continental extent, thousands of local moves can
vanish beneath a few international flows. At city scale, the same data may
reveal chain migration among neighbourhoods. There is no neutral zoom at which
“migration” appears.

</Section>

<Section id="archive-creates-migrant" title="The archive creates the migrant" rooms={['archive', 'border']}>

States and institutions create migrant categories for specific purposes:
passenger, alien, refugee, displaced person, guest worker, internal migrant,
resident, citizen. Categories can grant protection and resources. They can also
restrict movement, separate families and make some journeys deportable.

changes in how institutions made movement legible.

A census usually records residence at a reference moment. A border system
records encounters. A refugee registry records people inside a mandate and
procedure. Combining them without preserving definitions creates false
comparability. The same person may be visible in one system, absent from
another, and classified differently in a third.

The archive therefore does not merely measure migrants. It produces the unit
that can be measured.

</Section>

<Section id="route-is-not-straight" title="The route is not straight" rooms={['road', 'border']}>

Migration routes bend around visa rules, transport cost, family networks,
recruiters, seasonal work, conflict and weather. A destination can become a
pause; a pause can last years. Return may be planned, repeated or impossible.
The path is temporal as much as geographic.

Border policy redistributes danger. Closing one crossing may not end movement;
it can push journeys toward deserts, mountains or unsafe sea routes. A map that
shows only apprehensions after a policy change might mistake displaced
enforcement for increased movement.

Networks matter because knowledge travels through prior journeys. Relatives and
friends provide destinations, accommodation, warnings and employment leads.
These relations can sustain movement without appearing in transport data.
Their map is social: a set of trusted connections laid across physical routes.

Coercion further complicates the line. The Atlantic slave trade, deportation,
expulsion and forced resettlement cannot be folded into a neutral language of
flows. A map should name the institution that compelled movement and avoid
giving origin and destination equal agency.

</Section>

<Section id="absence-is-structured" title="Absence is structured" rooms={['archive', 'road']}>

Undocumented movement is not simply data missing at random. People may avoid
registration because a record creates risk. Remote deaths may never enter a
system. Women, children, mobile workers and people using informal routes can be
undercounted in different ways. Historical archives often preserve officials'
questions more reliably than travellers' experiences.

Blankness must therefore carry a reason. “No recorded crossings” is a statement
about the dataset. It is not “no one crossed.” A dashed line, uncertainty band
or note can show that evidence weakens; none should invent a route to fill the
silence.

Privacy introduces necessary absence. Detailed origin and destination pairs can
identify individuals in small groups. Sensitive routes can expose people to
enforcement or violence. Aggregation and spatial displacement may reduce harm,
but they also change the analysis. Those transformations belong in the
methodology.

Oral histories and participatory mapping can recover aspects of a journey, yet
they do not complete an objective master map. Memory is selective, and a
participant may reasonably withhold locations. Refusal is part of the evidence
boundary.

</Section>

<Section id="map-with-uncertainty" title="Map with uncertainty" rooms={['map', 'road', 'archive']}>

An uncertainty-aware migration map keeps at least four things separate:
recorded count, estimated count, inferred connection and documented route.
They should not share one unqualified line.

Time controls need definitions. A journey date, arrival date, registration date
and reporting date can differ. Filters should say which clock they use.
Categories need versioning so a legal change does not masquerade as a sudden
change in people.

Prose remains essential. Aggregate maps cannot explain motive at the individual
level. A rise in a flow may coincide with conflict, labour demand, family
reunification and policy change; the picture alone cannot assign shares of
causality.

The responsible map is sometimes less visually complete. It shows intervals,
unknowns and suppressed cells. It lets readers inspect definitions before
comparing totals. It gives no false path where the data contains only two
points.

Migration becomes visible through records, but visibility is never innocent.
The map should reveal the institution that saw the journey, the category
through which it was seen, and the people whom that way of seeing left in
motion beyond the page.

</Section>
