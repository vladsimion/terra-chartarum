## Two ways of holding the same journey in your head.

One is a list: London, then Dover, then a sea, then Beauvais, and so on to the
heel of Italy, with the days marked between. The other is a contract: so many
ships, so many marks, sailing on such a date, and if the money is not there then
neither is the crusade.

Both are maps in the sense this publication means. Neither is a map in the sense
of a picture of the ground.

And both are pointed at the same city, which is the third thing this essay has
to be careful about. Jerusalem in the thirteenth century was a destination, the
middle of the inhabited world, a text, a plan, a port two days' ride away, and -
for most of the years the road above was being drawn - a city the people drawing
it did not hold. Those are not versions of one fact. Keeping them apart is the
work.

<Section id="itinerarium" title="A road drawn as a list of days" rooms={['road', 'archive']}>

Matthew Paris drew the road from London to Otranto some time around the middle of
the thirteenth century. It runs up the page in columns: a town, a day-mark, a
town, a day-mark, for fourteen stages and something over a month.

It has no scale. It has no orientation. It has no coordinates. What it has is
order and duration, which for a traveller are the two facts that matter, and it
is worth resisting the reflex that calls this a primitive map. It is not a bad
picture of the ground. It is an excellent picture of a journey.

The sea crossing is the tell. Dover to Wissant is drawn in the same units as a
day's walk to Beauvais, because in the diagram's terms it _is_ the same kind of
thing: a stage that costs you a day. A map drawn to show the ground could not do
that. A map drawn to show a journey has to.

</Section>

<Section id="comparatio" title="Putting the diagram on a map" rooms={['map', 'road']}>

The Atlas layer for this act shows fourteen points, and every one of them is a
modern coordinate the manuscript does not supply.

That is a deliberate discomfort. Comparing the two is useful, and the comparison
is something we are doing rather than something the thirteenth century did. So
the data model refuses to let the two merge: a stage record has nowhere to put a
position at all, the coordinate belongs to the _place_ the stage names, and the
field carrying it is called `modernReference`.

The day-marks stay as day-marks. This project does not convert them into a travel
time, because the manuscript is making a claim about the journey and a
conversion would turn that claim into a measurement.

<ItineraryComparison />

Select a stage on either side and it lights up on both. What the two halves will
not do is become one picture, because a composite would be this project's
drawing rather than the thirteenth century's, and the difference between them is
the entire subject.

<Section id="terminus" title="Where the road stops" rooms={['road', 'border']}>

The last stage is Otranto, in the heel of Apulia, and the drawing ends there.

It ends there for a reason the diagram makes obvious once you look for it. The
itinerary's unit is a day, and it has already spent one of them on the crossing
from Dover, so it plainly can measure a sea. What it cannot do is measure _that_
sea. A fortnight of sailing depending on wind, convoy and season does not
decompose into stages with towns at the ends of them, and the diagram is honest
enough to stop rather than invent the ones it would need.

So the destination is carried without being drawn. The columns run in a
direction; the road stops at a sailing port; and everyone reading knew what was
on the far side. This is a strong claim about a document that has not been
transcribed here, and it is worth naming as an inference rather than a reading:
the register records the destination as a state of the itinerary and gives it no
position, because a pin in Judaea would be this project asserting what the
manuscript declines to draw.

What lies beyond Otranto is not a longer road. It is a shipping problem, and it
had been one for fifty years already.

</Section>

<Section id="centrum" title="The city in the middle of the world" rooms={['theatre', 'map']}>

Within a few years of the itinerary, an English psalter carried a world map the
size of a postcard, and Jerusalem is in the middle of it. A half-century later
the Hereford map does the same thing on a sheet of vellum well over a metre
across.

The reflex is to file this as an error corrected by later cartography, and the
reflex is wrong twice. It is wrong about the maps, which are not attempting the
kind of statement that could be wrong in that way. And it is wrong about what
centring does: a world image with Jerusalem in the middle is a claim about what
the world is arranged around, and there is no coordinate at which that claim
could be checked. The <EssayLink slug="invisible-maps-religion">religious world
images</EssayLink> in this collection are the long version of that argument; here
the point is narrower and structural.

Because it means the corpus cannot hold Jerusalem as a row. It has to hold it as
six rows of different kinds, and say what each kind may be used for.

<JerusalemRegisters />

Four of the ten records are on a layer, and the six that are not are the ones
doing most of the arguing. That asymmetry is the register working. A dataset
willing to draw all ten would have flattened a mappa mundi's middle, a text's
organising principle and a quay into the same object, which is the flattening
the whole essay is about.

One consequence lands immediately. For most of the years the itinerary was being
drawn, Jerusalem was not in Latin hands at all - it was lost in 1187, held again
briefly by treaty from 1229, and lost for good in 1244. The road above was drawn
to a city its readers could not enter. The sacred centre does not move when that
happens, because it was never the kind of thing that was there in the first
place.

</Section>

<Section id="contractus" title="The fleet that had to be paid for" rooms={['border', 'road']}>

Fifty years before Matthew Paris drew his road, a different kind of geography had
already been written down.

In 1201 the Fourth Crusade contracted with Venice for a fleet. The agreement
named the ships, the price and the destination, and the destination was Egypt.
The army that assembled could not pay. What followed was not a change of route so
much as a renegotiation, and the map of it is a map of terms rather than of
ground.

This is the shift the essay is named for. Jerusalem, in the twelfth century, was
the end of a road. By the thirteenth it was a node in a system of ports, credit
and shipping, and getting there had become a commercial problem before it was a
geographical one.

</Section>

<Section id="deviatio" title="Six things that are not one route" rooms={['border', 'map']}>

It is tempting to draw the Fourth Crusade as a line: Venice, Zara, Corfu,
Constantinople. The line would be wrong in an interesting way.

Six different things happened, and a route collapses them into one:

- the destination the crusade **contracted for**, which it never approached;
- the diversion it **negotiated**, which changed the terms rather than the
  position;
- the water it actually **travelled**, of which no source records a track;
- the two **assaults** on Constantinople, which were events at a place;
- the territory the Partitio Romaniae **assigned**, most of which the signatories
  did not hold;
- the territory anyone **durably controlled**, which was far less and was
  contested for fifty years.

The last two are the pair that matter. A treaty dividing an empire is a document
about intentions, and a map that draws the assignment in the same ink as the
occupation has republished the document's wishful thinking as geography. So the
partition carries no boundary in this dataset at all, and the possession is
pointed at the Venetian layers that already model it with their own evidence.

<FourthCrusadeStates />

Only the travelled route carries a line. Five of the six kinds are on no layer at
all, and that is the dataset agreeing with the argument rather than falling short
of it: a claim that was never a place cannot be given one without inventing it.

<Section id="portus" title="A sacred centre with a working port" rooms={['road', 'city']}>

The kingdom that called itself Jerusalem was, for most of the thirteenth century,
administered from Acre.

That sentence is the whole of the network argument in miniature. Pilgrims landed
at Jaffa and rode inland; grain, arms, money, officials and orders came through a
port two days up the coast; and the city at the middle of the world maps was
reached, supplied and governed through quays that appear on none of them. The
sacred geography and the working geography are both true, and they are not the
same map.

The Venetian Maritime Network is the other end of this. Those ports, their
phases, their evidence and their contested holdings are already published with
their own records, and this register points at them rather than re-authoring
them: Acre and Famagusta name `venetian-ports`, Alexandria names
`venetian-routes`. If you want the commercial system in its own right, read{' '}

<EssayLink slug="venice-sicily">Venice and Sicily</EssayLink>; what this essay takes from it is only
the shape.

The four ports outlive their reason. Acre fell in 1291 and the traffic moved to
Famagusta, on Cyprus, which is the network surviving the kingdom that gave it its
name. A crusading geography that ends in 1291 ends too early; the merchants did
not stop sailing because a war ended.

</Section>

<Section id="descriptio" title="The land written down, and ruled into squares" rooms={['archive', 'map']}>

Two objects from the end of this story do something the world maps and the
itinerary do not: they organise the Holy Land as a system.

The first is a description. Burchard of Mount Sion, a Dominican who lived in the
region, sets the land out in divisions running from a centre - and the centre he
takes them from is Acre, not Jerusalem. A description is a geography, and this
one quietly puts the port where the sacred city is on the mappae mundi. It is the
same substitution the previous act describes, made a second time in a different
medium, by someone who was standing there.

The second is a plan. Marino Sanudo Torsello, a Venetian, spent years pressing a
scheme on the papal court for recovering the Holy Land by cutting Egyptian trade,
and the copies of his treatise travel with maps in which the Holy Land is ruled
into a grid. It is the most modern-looking cartography in this corpus. The
squares carry no survey behind them and the expedition they were drawn for never
sailed, which makes the object a useful warning: the visual language of precision
arrives centuries before the measurement does, and it arrives in the service of
an argument. The catalogue holds two portolan sheets under the same maker's name,
read in <EssayLink slug="invisible-maps-trade">Invisible Maps of Trade</EssayLink>{' '}
as instruments of commerce - the same drafting practice, pointed at a crusade.

Neither object is reproduced here. Both need a rights review, and the register
that is about pictures of Jerusalem therefore shows no picture of Jerusalem. That
is a rights position rather than an editorial one, and the closing table names
it.

</Section>

<Section id="memoria" title="The centre outlives the kingdom" rooms={['theatre', 'archive']}>

In 1581, in Magdeburg, Heinrich Bünting printed the world as a clover leaf with
three continents for petals, meeting at Jerusalem.

Nearly three centuries after Acre fell, and a lifetime after the routes to the
Indies had been sailed, the centring survives - not as a mistake about geography
but as an emblem that had stopped needing to be about geography at all. That
survival is the last thing the essay wants to record, and recording it correctly
takes some care, because the temptation is to reach back with it. A
sixteenth-century woodcut that centres Jerusalem looks like evidence for how the
twelfth century thought.

It is not, and in this corpus it cannot become so. The record for the clover leaf
carries no source at all in the audit: it is joined to{' '}

<a href="/collection/religion-bunting/">its catalogue entry</a> and nothing else, so no crusading
claim can cite it. An early-modern map of the Holy Land is a witness to the early modern period.
Letting it stand as a witness to the medieval one is precisely the mistake this project exists to
avoid making tidily.

</Section>

<Section id="reliquum" title="What this essay cannot yet do" rooms={['archive', 'road']}>

The honest way to end is with the list.

The list used to be written here by hand, which meant it was a second copy of the
project's state and therefore the copy that went out of date. It is now compiled
from the same registers the validator reads.

<CrusadesGateState />

One gap changes what a reader sees, and it is worth naming outside the table: not
one folio or page has been transcribed. The comparison in the second act shows
the diagram's _sequence_ beside the ground and cannot show the diagram; the Holy
Land act argues about world images and shows none; and the two records resting on
Sanudo carry low confidence because what they cite is a treatise's general
position rather than a located passage.

There is a smaller item that belongs here for the same reason. The Levant places
in this corpus carry Arabic name forms, and those forms are romanisations taken
from published reference works rather than readings of an Arabic source. A schema
with a column for the name a place had for the people who lived there, filled in
from someone else's transliteration, is still telling a crusader's story in one
respect, and it is recorded as an open item rather than left as an impression of
thoroughness.

What exists is the argument and the machinery under it: a diagram that refuses to
become a map, a campaign that refuses to become a route, and a city that refuses
to become a coordinate. All three are driven entirely by the compiled dataset -
no route, date, day-mark or register is written into a component. When a witness
is cleared, the manuscript appears in the first of them and the world image in
the third, without the argument changing.

</Section>
