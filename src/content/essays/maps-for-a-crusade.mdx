## Two ways of holding the same journey in your head.

One is a list: London, then Dover, then a sea, then Beauvais, and so on to the
heel of Italy, with the days marked between. The other is a contract: so many
ships, so many marks, sailing on such a date, and if the money is not there then
neither is the crusade.

Both are maps in the sense this publication means. Neither is a map in the sense
of a picture of the ground.

<Section id="itinerarium" title="A road drawn as a list of days" rooms={['road', 'archive']}>

Matthew Paris drew the road from London to Otranto some time around the middle of
the thirteenth century. It runs up the page in columns: a town, a day-mark, a
town, a day-mark, for fourteen stages and something over a month.

It has no scale. It has no orientation. It has no coordinates. What it has is
order and duration, which for a traveller are the two facts that matter, and it
is worth resisting the reflex that calls this a primitive map. It is not a bad
picture of the ground. It is an excellent picture of a journey.

The sea crossing is the tell. Dover to Wissant is drawn in the same units as a
day's walk to Beauvais, because in the diagram's terms it _is_ the same kind of
thing: a stage that costs you a day. A map drawn to show the ground could not do
that. A map drawn to show a journey has to.

</Section>

<Section id="comparatio" title="Putting the diagram on a map" rooms={['map', 'road']}>

The Atlas layer for this act shows fourteen points, and every one of them is a
modern coordinate the manuscript does not supply.

That is a deliberate discomfort. Comparing the two is useful, and the comparison
is something we are doing rather than something the thirteenth century did. So
the data model refuses to let the two merge: a stage record has nowhere to put a
position at all, the coordinate belongs to the _place_ the stage names, and the
field carrying it is called `modernReference`.

The day-marks stay as day-marks. This project does not convert them into a travel
time, because the manuscript is making a claim about the journey and a
conversion would turn that claim into a measurement.

</Section>

<Section id="contractus" title="The fleet that had to be paid for" rooms={['border', 'road']}>

Fifty years before Matthew Paris drew his road, a different kind of geography had
already been written down.

In 1201 the Fourth Crusade contracted with Venice for a fleet. The agreement
named the ships, the price and the destination, and the destination was Egypt.
The army that assembled could not pay. What followed was not a change of route so
much as a renegotiation, and the map of it is a map of terms rather than of
ground.

This is the shift the flagship essay is about. Jerusalem, in the twelfth century,
was the end of a road. By the thirteenth it was a node in a system of ports,
credit and shipping, and getting there had become a commercial problem before it
was a geographical one.

</Section>

<Section id="deviatio" title="Four things that are not one route" rooms={['border', 'map']}>

It is tempting to draw the Fourth Crusade as a line: Venice, Zara, Corfu,
Constantinople. The line would be wrong in an interesting way.

Six different things happened, and a route collapses them into one:

- the destination the crusade **contracted for**, which it never approached;
- the diversion it **negotiated**, which changed the terms rather than the
  position;
- the water it actually **travelled**, of which no source records a track;
- the two **assaults** on Constantinople, which were events at a place;
- the territory the Partitio Romaniae **assigned**, most of which the signatories
  did not hold;
- the territory anyone **durably controlled**, which was far less and was
  contested for fifty years.

The last two are the pair that matter. A treaty dividing an empire is a document
about intentions, and a map that draws the assignment in the same ink as the
occupation has republished the document's wishful thinking as geography. So the
partition carries no boundary in this dataset at all, and the possession is
pointed at the Venetian layers that already model it with their own evidence.

</Section>

<Section id="reliquum" title="What this prototype does not yet do" rooms={['archive', 'road']}>

This is a prototype, and the honest way to end it is with the list.

No folio has been transcribed. Every locator in the Crusades corpus reads
`pending`, and the rights terms for the digitised manuscripts have not been read,
so no image is reproduced here and none may be. The manuscript-to-modern
comparison the Road proof is for cannot yet show the manuscript.

The twenty core places are compiled and not reviewed. The route lines are
generalisations between ports. The full flagship needs Acre and the Holy Land
records, the sacred geography the itinerary was aiming at, and the century of
maritime infrastructure between the two proofs.

What exists is the argument and the machinery under it: a diagram that refuses to
become a map, and a campaign that refuses to become a route.

</Section>
