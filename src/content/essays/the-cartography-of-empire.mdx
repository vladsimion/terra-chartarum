# The Cartography of Empire

## The administrative sheet

An empire is difficult to see. Authority is dispersed through councils,
governors, tax farms, courts, garrisons, privileges, roads and negotiated local
custom. It is uneven by design and in practice. The administrative map answers
that difficulty with a surface divided into named units. What cannot be
inspected all at once in the world can be arranged all at once on paper.

Seventeenth-century maps associated with Nicolas Sanson and eighteenth-century
atlases published by Antonio Zatta sit at a revealing hinge. Their sheets are
not modern bureaucratic GIS, and they should not be recruited into a simple
origin story for the nation-state. Yet they show how commercial atlas making,
geographic compilation and territorial classification could make rule
repeatable. A province becomes an outline that can be copied, coloured, indexed
and placed beside another.

This essay reads those sheets as administrative arguments. It asks not whether
every boundary was surveyed to modern standards, but what the boundary allowed
an atlas to claim.

<Section id="empire-becomes-a-sheet" title="Empire becomes a sheet" rooms={['border', 'map']}>

The sheet imposes a practical frame. A title names the object; a neatline
separates it from the rest of the world; a scale, graticule and place hierarchy
make its contents comparable. Colour can distinguish adjacent jurisdictions
even when the printed line is shared. The result is an object that looks
complete enough to file.

This completeness is made. Imperial jurisdictions overlap. A bishopric, tax
district and military command may divide the same region differently. Towns may
hold privileges that interrupt a uniform provincial story. Frontier authority
may weaken gradually rather than stop at a stroke. Selecting one division for
the sheet does not merely simplify the empire. It declares which administrative
relation deserves to represent it.

Atlas pages intensify the claim through repetition. Turn the leaf and another
territory appears in the same visual grammar. The sequence suggests that
political space is naturally composed of bounded, named and mutually comparable
units. The atlas does not need to announce a theory of empire; its format
performs one.

<Section id="sanson-classifies" title="Sanson classifies" rooms={['archive', 'border']}>

Nicolas Sanson is often described as a founder of French cartography. The label
can hide the more interesting operation visible in his titles: geography is
organised through divisions. Ancient and modern regions, ecclesiastical and
political units, kingdoms and provinces can each become the governing subject
of a map.

Classification happens before a reader follows any line. The title determines
which spatial order will count. The sheet then sorts names by typography,
chooses which settlements receive symbols, and makes some boundaries more
legible than others. Even uncertainty acquires a graphic place-as a faint line,
an incomplete coast, a note, or silence.

Sanson's work circulated through copying, partnership and reissue. That
afterlife matters because administrative cartography gains force through stable
reuse. Once a division has a recognisable plate and title, later publishers can
correct, embellish or inherit it. The archive preserves not a sequence of wholly
new maps but a lineage of territorial propositions.

The appropriate critical question is therefore not “Did Sanson invent this
border?” It is “Which source, classification and publishing chain allowed this
border to remain available?” That question returns agency to compilation,
engraving and atlas commerce without pretending the paper line directly
governed the land.

</Section>

<Section id="zatta-inherits" title="Zatta inherits and revises" rooms={['archive', 'city']}>

Antonio Zatta's Venetian publishing programme assembled geographic knowledge
for a late eighteenth-century market. His _Atlante Novissimo_ belongs to a
world of translated texts, reworked plates, commercial competition and
political change. Its administrative sheets often carry earlier geographic
structures into a new decorative and editorial setting.

Inheritance is not passivity. A publisher chooses what to retain, what to
rename, how to divide a series and which current authorities to cite. A border
copied from an earlier model enters a different present. New titles and
colouring can make old geography answer new questions.

Venice gives the problem particular force. The Republic's mainland and maritime
domains were not one homogeneous block. Cities, islands and coastal holdings
entered Venetian rule through different histories and retained different
institutions. A Zatta sheet can collect them as a visual unit; the clean view is
an editorial accomplishment, not an x-ray of government.

Reading Zatta beside Sanson exposes the atlas as an archive of reuse. Similar
coastlines do not prove identical politics. Changed labels do not guarantee
changed geometry. The meaningful object is often the seam between inherited
plate knowledge and revised administrative language.

</Section>

<Section id="border-does-administrative-work" title="The border does administrative work" rooms={['border', 'city']}>

A boundary on a small-scale atlas cannot enforce a tax or stop a traveller.
Nevertheless, it performs administrative work by making a jurisdiction
referable. A reader can point to it, compare it, teach it, index it and place it
inside a larger hierarchy. The map turns distributed practices into an object
that can circulate beyond the offices that produced them.

That work has exclusions. Enclaves may vanish at page scale. Shared rights
become a single fill. Seasonal access, linguistic difference and local
privilege rarely receive the graphic weight of an external border. The map
favours the relations most compatible with a clean partition.

The city reveals the cost. Administrative sheets often reduce cities to ranked
symbols while using those same cities as capitals, courts and anchors for
regional names. Urban institutions make the territory governable, yet the
territorial image can make the city appear secondary to the surface it
organises.

This reversal is not an error to be corrected with more detail. It is the
sheet's argument: authority can be represented as area. Once that proposition
is visible, the map becomes evidence for how empire wanted to be legible, not
only for where its line was drawn.

</Section>

<Section id="read-the-seams" title="Read the seams" rooms={['archive', 'map']}>

To read an administrative map against the grain, begin with the frame. What
unit has been made to fit? Then inspect the hierarchy of names and symbols.
Which places organise the territory, and which survive only as texture? Compare
editions and publishers. Where does a plate retain old geography beneath new
labels? Finally, place the sheet beside records produced for another purpose:
an itinerary, a judicial district, a port network, a cadastral plan.

The disagreements are productive. They show that empire had several shapes at
once. The administrative map is not discredited when another source refuses
its partition. It becomes more precisely historical: one technology for making
layered authority look bounded, repeatable and available at a glance.

That precision also disciplines present-day reuse. Digitising the line does not
make it more certain. A vector polygon can conceal an inherited engraving as
easily as colour once did. Historical GIS should therefore preserve the sheet's
title, date, publisher, source lineage and scale of claim. The border needs a
provenance record as much as a coordinate.

</Section>
