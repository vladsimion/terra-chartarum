# The League That Left No Map

## The Hansa connected half a continent, but possessed no shape that a map could easily hold.

Draw northern Europe in the later Middle Ages and one temptation arrives before the ink is dry.
Give the Hanseatic League a colour. Fill the towns that appear in a modern list, join them with
lines, and let the resulting web stand for a political body. The picture will be immediately
recognisable. It will also be too certain in almost every way that matters.

The Hanse was not a kingdom with a stable frontier, a republic with one sovereign council, or a
corporation with a surviving roll of members. It gathered merchants, towns, regional groupings,
assemblies, overseas establishments, treaties, privileges and habits of cooperation that changed
over centuries. A city could attend one Diet and not another. Merchants from a town could trade
within the network without establishing a constitutional status that a modern database can safely
call membership. A Kontor could exercise serious internal discipline while remaining inside the
law and political authority of London, Bergen, Bruges or Novgorod. The vocabulary of a modern
political map - border, capital, province, colony - therefore hides the mechanism it claims to reveal.

This essay begins elsewhere: with witnesses that show cities, seas and documents but do not show a
single Hanseatic territory. It treats absence as evidence of a mismatch between institution and
cartographic form, not as proof that contemporaries failed to understand their commercial world.
The maps knew how to depict the world they were made to depict. The problem is ours when we ask
one image to perform the work of a privilege charter, an ordinance, a merchant oath, a city view
and a route history at once.

The interactive diagrams below are modern analytical reconstructions built from the published HSE
datasets. Their lines are generalized relationships between documented nodes. Their dates are
evidence windows, not a concealed membership list. Their commodity filters record qualitative
associations, not cargo quantities. Whenever the historical sources support less certainty than the
screen can display, the text and legend keep that limitation visible.

<Section id="prologue-remove-network" title="Remove the network" rooms={['road', 'archive', 'theatre']}>

Open the _Rudimentum Novitiorum_ at folios 85 verso and 86 recto. Printed in Lübeck by Lucas
Brandis in 1475, the double-page woodcut gives a world organized through inherited universal
history. Regions become hills or compartments. Cities rise as signs. East, south and north orient a
cosmos in which sacred, historical and geographical order meet. Northern names are present:
Gothia, Livonia, Dacia, Frisia, England and Muscovy belong to the printed world. What is absent is
the commercial system surrounding the press that made the book.

There are no Hanseatic routes, no Kontore, no warehouses and no visual grammar for a privilege
negotiated in another ruler’s city. This is not a complaint that a world chronicle failed to become
a trade atlas. It is a precise observation about a specific opening. A map printed at a leading
Hanseatic centre could make the whole world meaningful without turning the Hansa into a visible
object. The source therefore establishes a problem of representation, not a proposition that
fifteenth-century merchants lacked geographical knowledge. [Evidence:
`hse-claim-lubeck-worldmap-not-a-trade-map`](#hse-claim-lubeck-worldmap-not-a-trade-map).

Now place a modern network over that opening. Lübeck connects east through Visby toward Novgorod,
north toward Bergen, and west through North Sea cities toward London and Bruges. The lines make a
relationship legible in seconds. They also import a powerful fiction: that the relationship existed
as one simultaneous, bounded and measurable thing. A clean overlay tends to erase the difference
between an itinerary and a treaty, a repeated commercial connection and a traced voyage, a
merchant community and a town’s participation in collective action.

The responsible reconstruction must therefore be removable. When the network disappears, the
historical witness is not reduced to a blank background. Its own categories return. The
_Rudimentum_ orders universal space, salvation history and accumulated geographic names. It does
not wait passively for a twenty-first-century data layer to complete it. When the network returns,
it should be read as our question laid over the source: where did relationships operate that this
cartographic genre had no reason to draw?

<NetworkReveal />

The alternation matters because visualisation often makes analytical convenience look like
historical presence. A line has endpoints, a stroke width and a continuous path. The evidence may
support only repeated connection between places across a broad period. The HSE route table answers
that problem by labelling every corridor with an evidence type and certainty term. The diagram
answers it by keeping the historical witness and the reconstruction as different modes rather than
blending them into one seamless authority.

The Hansa left many maps in the ordinary sense: maps printed in its cities, maps used by merchants,
charts of the seas through which ships moved, city views of the urban centres involved. What it did
not leave was one contemporary map that corresponds to the modern desire for “the Hanseatic League”
as a single territorial object. The absence is productive. It tells us to look for operative
geography in other records and to return to maps with a different question.

</Section>

<Section id="merchants-before-league" title="Merchants before the League" rooms={['road', 'archive']}>

Begin this time with a rule book. The Second Novgorod _Schra_, preserved in a Riga copy dated before
1298, contains sixty-four articles for the overseas merchant community at Peterhof. Its world is
made from oaths, mutual aid, duties and internal procedure. It offers no founding scene in which a
League suddenly appears. Instead it shows merchants organizing life abroad and later encountering
the growing regulatory reach of interurban assemblies.

The word _hansa_ itself moved through this history. Modern scholarship traces it from communities
of long-distance merchants toward later use for “the Hanse,” while warning that a change in
language does not reveal a clean constitutional birth. Towns became involved through their
merchants; merchant groupings and town cooperation overlapped rather than succeeding one another
like governments in a chronology. [Evidence: `hse-claim-hanse-term-shift`](#hse-claim-hanse-term-shift).
The Second _Schra_ supports the same layered reading: an oath-bound voluntary union existed before
and alongside later Diet regulation. [Evidence:
`hse-claim-merchant-to-town-regulation`](#hse-claim-merchant-to-town-regulation).

That distinction changes how an origin story should be mapped. A conventional animation might
begin with Lübeck, send a line to Visby, extend it to Novgorod and label the final frame “the
League.” The sequence would turn a useful narrative corridor into a constitutional diagram. It
would also imply that places nearer the line belonged earlier or more fully than places outside it.
The sources permit a more modest claim: merchants, settlements and routes in this eastern corridor
formed important contexts for cooperation; they do not supply a single institutional birth track.

The problem becomes sharper around 1356. That year is often used for the beginning of regular
general Diets, while the surviving copied series of recesses begins in 1361. The project records a
1356–1361 evidence window rather than naming an uncontested first Hansetag. The modern synthesis
also rejects the old story in which a merchant Hanse was simply replaced by a town Hanse at this
moment. [Evidence: `hse-claim-hansetag-documentation-window`](#hse-claim-hansetag-documentation-window).
There is an institutional change to describe, but no need to convert it into a foundation ceremony.

This is why the gazetteer avoids `member_city`. The term sounds harmless, yet it presumes a stable
body that could admit, record and remove members in a consistent way. The surviving evidence is
more uneven. Attendance at a Diet, representation through another town, participation in a regional
quarter, commercial association and the presence of merchants in an overseas establishment are
different relationships. The HSE data keeps them different. Its broad AD 1356–1669 phases are
analytical windows for visualisation, not claims that sixty towns possessed one unbroken status for
313 years.

The route from Lübeck through Visby to Novgorod remains valuable when its purpose is stated. It
allows the reader to move between a leading convening city, a Baltic market and an overseas
merchant compound. It gathers evidence that otherwise sits in separate archives. It helps explain
why the Hansa is best approached through movement and institutional layering. What it cannot do is
serve as the League’s spine, border or birth certificate.

At Peterhof, the rules show a community substantial enough to discipline its participants. They
also show the limits of that autonomy. The merchants lived and traded within Novgorod’s political
world. Their compound was not a detached province. The discipline of the _Schra_ was real; so was
the dependence of the establishment on relations with its host. That combination - internal order
without territorial sovereignty - will recur in every principal Kontor.

</Section>

<Section id="four-cities-inside-other-cities" title="Four cities inside other cities" rooms={['city', 'road', 'archive']}>

The London plate in the 1593 _Civitates Orbis Terrarum_ does something unusual. Beside its view of
the city, a printed block headed “Stilliards” describes the Hansa as a confederated society of many
cities. It says that the association has four emporia, which some call Kontore, where the cities’
merchants reside; it then names the London house as the _Gildehalla Teutonica_, commonly called the
Steelyard. The plate supplies a contemporary printed vocabulary for an institution the skyline
alone cannot explain. [Evidence:
`hse-claim-hansa-confoederata-societas`](#hse-claim-hansa-confoederata-societas) and
`hse-claim-four-kontore-attested`](#hse-claim-four-kontore-attested).

Those four overseas establishments - Novgorod, Bergen, London and Bruges - were not four colonies in a
Hanseatic empire. They were merchant communities embedded in cities governed by others. Their
privileges, spatial arrangements and internal regulations differed. Even “merchant compound,” the
project’s controlled term, is a practical comparison rather than a claim that their physical forms
were identical. What joins them is an institutional problem: how could merchants maintain a
recognisable communal order abroad while depending on host-city and host-ruler authority?

Peterhof answers through the Second _Schra_. Its articles bind merchants by oath, require mutual
aid and organize conduct. Later Diet intervention demonstrates that the community’s internal rules
were connected to a wider interurban order. Yet nothing in the selected evidence turns Peterhof
into Hanseatic territory. The dossier uses AD 1295–1494 for the phase attested by the ordinance and
its institutional afterlife, ending with the documented suppression of the establishment. Neither
bound is promoted into a beginning or ending for the wider Hanse. [Evidence:
`hse-claim-novgorod-second-schra`](#hse-claim-novgorod-second-schra).

<KontorProfile kontorId="novgorod" />

Bryggen answers through its harbour fabric and its court books. Warehouses, offices, dwellings,
assembly rooms, kitchens and stone cellars made the community materially durable. Norwegian Crown
privileges supported an internal commercial court, communal contributions, fire rules, elected
officers and annual price lists. Such autonomy could be considerable without becoming sovereignty.
The court books continue from 1633 to 1759; the last secretary left in 1761 and the archive moved to
Lübeck in 1764. Bergen therefore refuses the idea that the general Diet of 1669 ended every
Hanseatic institution at once. [Evidence:
`hse-claim-bergen-court-and-afterlife`](#hse-claim-bergen-court-and-afterlife).

<KontorProfile kontorId="bergen" />

The Steelyard answers through a city view and thirty leaves of by-laws. The London plate locates the
merchant house inside the metropolis and beside the river world on which its trade depended. The
1554 ordinance regulates elections, internal jurisdiction, finance, conduct and training for a
community described in the dossier as representing sixty-six towns. This was meaningful corporate
self-government, but its foundation remained English privilege. Suppression in 1598 demonstrates
the consequence: host authorities could close the selected institutional phase. [Evidence:
`hse-claim-london-bylaws-1554`](#hse-claim-london-bylaws-1554).

<KontorProfile kontorId="london" />

Bruges answers through a dispersed documentary geography. Charters granted building rights at
Oosterlingenplein and access to a House of Nations in 1458, staple rights in 1487 and reduced excise
on Rhenish wine in 1502. Each concession connected a commercial activity to a place and a legal
condition. None detached that place from the city’s authority. The dossier’s AD 1458–1502 span is
therefore the window of the selected charter group, not the life of Hanseatic presence in Bruges.
[Evidence: `hse-claim-bruges-chartered-rights`](#hse-claim-bruges-chartered-rights).

<KontorProfile kontorId="bruges" />

Together the four profiles reveal a geography inside geography. A merchant could cross a gate,
enter a hall, sleep in a compound, submit to rules, elect officers, store goods and resolve disputes
within an institution recognisable to colleagues from distant towns. Yet every one of those acts
took place in a host political world. The Kontor was powerful precisely because it translated
between internal organisation and external privilege. Calling it a colony erases that translation
and replaces negotiated access with a territorial fiction.

</Section>

<Section id="city-is-unit" title="The city is the unit" rooms={['city', 'road']}>

Look at the shared Lübeck and Hamburg plate in the _Civitates Orbis Terrarum_. Each city is given a
portrait, name, arms, waterfront and civic claim. Lübeck’s cartouche calls it an imperial free city
and head of the Wendish cities and the illustrious Hanseatic society. That wording is valuable as
evidence of how a 1593 printed source described the city; it is not an independent constitutional
proof that all Hanseatic authority radiated from one capital. [Evidence:
`hse-claim-lubeck-societatis-caput`](#hse-claim-lubeck-societatis-caput).

The city view is exceptionally good at making civic bodies appear coherent. Walls and waterways
hold a settlement together. Towers and churches organise the skyline. Ships approach a named
harbour. A title and heraldic device turn a dense urban field into a political individual. The
Hansa, by contrast, appears indirectly. It can be named in a cartouche or descriptive paragraph,
suggested by ships and warehouses, or inferred from relationships among several plates. It does
not acquire a shared wall.

This difference explains why the city is the most useful unit in the gazetteer. Lübeck, Hamburg,
Bremen, Cologne, Gdańsk, Riga and dozens of other places can be located without pretending that
their relationship created one territory. Their roles can be described: leading city, active city,
represented town, market, Kontor host, foreign branch or commercial associate. Their participation
terms can preserve uncertainty. Their historic and modern names can remain searchable. The Atlas
can then assemble a network view for a selected year without baking a constitutional judgment into
the coordinate itself.

The Cologne plate demonstrates why careful naming matters. Its text speaks of a league of
seventy-two cities and says Cologne was reckoned among four principal cities. The London plate, in
the same volume, speaks of four Kontore. These are two different fours: one concerns a contemporary
printed description of principal cities, the other overseas emporia. A map caption that says only
“the four Hanseatic centres” would collapse separate institutional categories into a neat but false
hierarchy.

City portraits can also carry chronology in their grammar. The 1593 Bruges plate describes the
city as having once been a most flourishing emporium. The past tense supports a narrative of lost
commercial primacy without supplying a single date for decline. [Evidence:
`hse-claim-bruges-emporium-past-tense`](#hse-claim-bruges-emporium-past-tense). The difference is
important. A source can remember former greatness while the institutions associated with that
greatness continue, relocate or change. Decline is not one switch that the map allows us to date.

The witness strip makes this civic fragmentation visible. Most entries are city views from one
rights-cleared Heidelberg volume. That concentration is itself part of the source story: six
production witnesses depend on one repository and one digitised book. The interface therefore
links to catalogue records carrying repository IDs and rights statements rather than presenting
the plates as anonymous illustrations.

<CityWitnessStrip />

A city-based model does not claim that cities acted alone. Merchants, rulers, guilds, regional
groupings and foreign communities appear throughout the evidence. It claims only that the city is
a safer cartographic unit than an invented Hanseatic province. The city can be plotted, inspected
and compared while the relationships around it remain dated and qualified. In this sense the city
view’s limitation becomes a methodological advantage: it refuses to supply the common border that
the institution never possessed.

</Section>

<Section id="goods-draw-different-leagues" title="Goods draw different leagues" rooms={['road', 'city']}>

On the four-city plate that includes Bremen, the Lüneburg panel is captioned as a noble Saxon city
famous for its salt works. That small textual claim points beyond civic portraiture. Salt links an
inland production centre to Lübeck and to eastern corridors where it appears among the goods moving
toward Novgorod. Select a different commodity and a different configuration emerges. Stockfish
pulls the eye toward Bergen. Cloth connects western markets and eastern demand. Grain makes river
and maritime passages legible. Wine and metals gather another set of relations around the Rhine
and London.

There was never one commodity network identical to “the Hansa.” Each family depended on different
origins, seasons, intermediaries, privileges and destinations. A merchant association could be
strong along one chain and weak along another. Towns that look peripheral in a diagram of fish may
be central to salt or cloth. A generalized network becomes more historically useful when it can be
redrawn by goods, provided that the redrawing does not pretend to measure what the sources do not
measure.

The HSE commodity table therefore contains families rather than cargo totals. Salt groups
preserving and industrial uses without tonnage. Cloth joins finished and semi-finished textiles
without claiming that grades and origins were interchangeable. Grain avoids crop-specific
quantities. Timber gathers boards and forest products at corridor level. Wax, fur, stockfish,
herring, wine, metals and beer follow the same rule: the category says that a relation is evidenced,
not how much passed through it.

This restraint is especially important for the later Sound Toll data that informs the modern
network study used by the project. Those flow records begin after several medieval and early-modern
phases displayed here, and their coverage has structural omissions. Projecting their quantities
back into AD 1356, 1458 or 1554 would turn excellent later evidence into fabricated earlier volume.
The HSE model uses that scholarship for identifiers, network context and carefully bounded
associations. It does not back-project numbers.

<CommodityNetworkMap />

The Lübeck–Visby–Novgorod corridor illustrates the result. Its four commodity families include
cloth and salt moving east and fur and wax moving west at a qualitative level. The path is a
generalized mixed maritime and riverine reconstruction joining documented nodes. It is not an
individual voyage. The Lübeck–Bergen corridor associates stockfish moving south and selected
return-cargo families moving north, again without a share or total. Hamburg–London and
Cologne–London represent different western relations even when both terminate in the same host
city.

The interface keeps every line the same weight. That visual decision is not merely aesthetic. A
thicker line would invite the reader to infer volume. Animation speed would invite an inference
about frequency or journey time. Direction arrows could imply a stable one-way flow where the
underlying category is mixed. Instead the filter performs a simpler operation: show which authored
corridors carry a normalized join to the selected commodity family, and disclose the evidence type,
certainty and dates beside each route.

Goods thus draw different leagues without defining separate constitutions. They reveal overlapping
fields of dependence and access. Stockfish cannot explain the place of Cologne; Rhenish wine cannot
explain Peterhof; salt cannot by itself explain the institutional endurance of Bryggen. Taken
together, the families show why the Hansa was both coherent and difficult to bound. Its geography
changed with the question being asked.

</Section>

<Section id="power-without-sovereignty" title="Power without sovereignty" rooms={['border', 'road', 'archive']}>

Two treaty instruments dated 24 May 1370 record the Peace of Stralsund. They followed conflict in
which the Cologne Confederation, overlapping only partly with the wider Hanse, confronted Denmark.
The settlement renewed and expanded privileges and is often interpreted as a political high-water
mark. The date is secure; the meaning needs qualification. A coalition’s success cannot be made
identical to a permanent Hanseatic state. [Evidence:
`hse-claim-stralsund-1370`](#hse-claim-stralsund-1370).

The event shows power operating through access. The outcome mattered because privileges, trading
conditions and the ability to move through a political maritime world could change. None of that
required the victors to annex a continuous northern territory. Power could be exerted by
coordination, embargo, negotiation, compensation, threat and the temporary withdrawal of merchants.
Its map was a field of permitted and obstructed relations.

The Flanders embargo of 1388 makes the limits equally visible. The Diet ordered merchants to leave
Flanders and prohibited trade with Flemish merchants or goods that had passed through the county.
The evidence also records widespread evasion. A legal act could attempt to close access without
perfectly controlling behaviour. When merchants returned from Dordrecht to Bruges in December 1392,
they did so after compensation and renewed concessions. The sequence is neither territorial conquest
nor simple compliance. It is negotiated commercial geography unfolding through imperfect collective
action.

Privilege could be granted, confirmed, narrowed or suppressed. The Bruges charters locate rights in
buildings, staples and excise. The London by-laws sit within privileges vulnerable to English
decisions. Peterhof could be suppressed in 1494. The Steelyard’s selected late phase could be closed
in 1598. Each event changes the network’s usable geography without redrawing the sovereignty of
Europe.

<PrivilegeTimeline />

The timeline also resists a smooth rise-and-fall story. Sixteenth-century reorganisation and
political fragmentation coincided with economic strength in parts of the network. The 1554 and 1557
changes were not merely the final movement of a dying institution. [Evidence:
`hse-claim-decline-not-linear`](#hse-claim-decline-not-linear). Different cities, routes and
establishments followed different trajectories. Printed cartography of the north could become more
detailed as the association’s collective political position became less commanding.

This is the point at which a territory layer would do the most damage. Filling a Hanseatic polygon
for 1370 would make diplomatic success look like sovereign possession. Shrinking it after 1494 or
1598 would make the closure of overseas establishments look like lost provinces. Leaving the
territory view empty is not a visual failure. It is the historically substantive choice. The proper
alternatives are layers for places, corridors and institutional events, each with its own temporal
and evidentiary contract.

Power without sovereignty is not weak power. Merchants could secure durable privileges, organise
collective responses and build institutions whose records survived for centuries. Towns could
coordinate war and peace. Overseas communities could regulate conduct and adjudicate disputes. The
qualification concerns the form of power: substantial effects did not require a single sovereign
territory, and the map should not grant one after the fact.

</Section>

<Section id="charter-is-a-map" title="The charter is a map" rooms={['archive', 'road']}>

Take the Bruges charter group as a spatial document. A grant in 1458 concerns building rights at
Oosterlingenplein and a House of Nations. A 1487 charter concerns staple rights for merchandise
moving through Flanders and Brabant. A 1502 concession reduces excise on Rhenish wine. Read in
sequence, the documents define places, goods, obligations and routes of access more precisely than
a small-scale map of northern Europe could.

A charter is not literally a cartographic image. Yet it can perform cartographic work. It names
where an activity may occur, who may undertake it, which authority grants permission, what payment
is modified and how movement through a jurisdiction is structured. The operative geography lies in
conditions. Remove the conditions from a route line and the line becomes an abstract possibility.
Restore them and the route becomes a historically situated permission.

The London ordinance performs a related operation inside the merchant community. Elections,
finance, jurisdiction, conduct and training organize a social space. The rules make the Steelyard
more than a warehouse marker. They tell us how a group reproduced itself, disciplined participants
and managed common affairs. The city view provides location and public description; the ordinance
provides procedures. Neither source is complete, but their difference is productive.

Peterhof’s _Schra_ reaches further back. Its oath and mutual-aid provisions turn a group of
travelling merchants into a regulated community without creating a sovereign enclave. Later Diet
intervention writes interurban authority into that local rule system. The document therefore maps
institutional scale: participant, merchant compound, leading legal tradition and collective town
assembly overlap in one record.

Bryggen’s court books extend the method through time. Annual entries about disputes, fees, safety,
office holders and prices make continuity visible at a resolution no general map can match. They
also reveal why end dates must attach to specific institutions. A last general Diet in 1669 does not
close court books that continue to 1759. The departure of a last secretary in 1761 and transfer of
an archive in 1764 are Bergen events, not universal dissolution dates.

Archives pose a visual challenge. The most important documentary objects are not always available
under terms that permit reproduction. The Peterhof copy, Bergen court books and London ordinance
are identified to repositories and shelfmarks, but their archival images remain restricted. The
Bruges charters are treated as public-domain objects, yet a direct repository surrogate has not
been secured. The catalogue therefore publishes metadata, rights status and evidence without
smuggling a convenient image from a dealer or an unlicensed reproduction into the essay.

This absence must remain visible too. A polished visual essay can create pressure to fill every
section with an image. Doing so would make licensing convenience govern historical importance. The
better approach distinguishes a rights-cleared visual witness from a reference-only documentary
record. A reader can inspect the citation, source key, page or folio locator and publication status
even where no image appears.

The charter is a map, then, in two senses. Historically, it organizes access through words rather
than lines. Editorially, it directs the project toward a responsible representation of what can be
shown, what can only be described and what remains to be cleared. The archive does not sit behind
the map as supporting detail. It supplies the operative geography that the map cannot carry.

</Section>

<Section id="sea-becomes-legible" title="The sea becomes legible as the institution fragments" rooms={['map', 'road']}>

Olaus Magnus’s _Carta Marina_ of 1539 makes the northern seas abundant. Coasts, islands, towns,
ships, animals, currents of attention and regions of wonder crowd a monumental printed surface. The
north is no longer a sparse edge of a universal diagram. It has become a richly differentiated
world that invites movement and comparison.

That new visibility does not amount to a map of the Hansa. The _Carta Marina_ shows places and
maritime life, not a constitutional network. Its importance here lies in historical juxtaposition.
Northern cartography grows more elaborate during centuries in which Hanseatic institutions adapt,
compete and fragment. Better mapped seas do not produce a clearer outline for an association whose
power still rests in relations rather than common territory.

Later sixteenth-century charts and atlases sharpen the paradox. Cornelis Anthonisz’s _Caerte van
Oostlant_, Ortelius’s northern regional sheets and Waghenaer’s _Spieghel der Zeevaerdt_ belong to a
world of increasingly standardised printed navigation and regional description. Dutch competition,
territorial states and changing commercial structures altered the conditions under which Hanseatic
collective power operated. The sea becomes more legible at the same time that the institution is
less easily narrated as a single dominant arc.

The 1593 city plates contribute another kind of legibility. Lübeck, Hamburg, London, Bruges,
Cologne and Bremen can be compared as visible urban bodies. Their texts name commercial roles and
institutional claims. Yet the volume still requires a reader to assemble the association across
separate pages. The Hansa exists between images: in shared vocabulary, linked goods, repeated
privileges and remembered status.

This is why an analytical Atlas remains useful. It can put the HSE place, route and event layers on
one temporal interface; allow historic and modern names to be searched; preserve a selected feature
in a deep link; and expose source metadata in a panel. It can do what no one historical map was made
to do. Its authority, however, depends on refusing to imitate a recovered original. The Atlas is a
research instrument assembled from distinct evidence classes.

The difference is visible in line style and language. A documented repeated connection can be
distinguished from a generalized reconstruction. Medium certainty can be dashed rather than merely
coloured. Event points carry actors and targets instead of becoming anonymous dates. Place roles
remain categories of evidence and participation, not ranks in a state hierarchy. A user who cannot
perceive colour can still read the distinctions; a user who turns off motion loses no information.

As cartography becomes more capable of describing the sea, it also teaches a caution. Richness of
display is not completeness of institution. A chart may know harbours and hazards while omitting
privileges. A city atlas may know walls and quays while omitting commodity chains. A network diagram
may know relationships while omitting the textual instruments that made them lawful. Every form
clarifies by selecting.

</Section>

<Section id="epilogue-sea-between-cities" title="A sea between cities" rooms={['road', 'city', 'archive']}>

Return finally to the Bruges view of 1593. Its text remembers a city that had once been a most
flourishing emporium. The image itself remains full, ordered and dignified. Former commercial
primacy does not appear as a ruined gap in the engraved city. Decline enters through tense, through
comparison with charters and relocations, and through knowledge of relationships that the plate
cannot draw.

The last general Hanseatic Diet in 1669 offers a similar editorial temptation. It is a useful
terminus for the essay because it closes the regular general-assembly sequence used by the project.
It is not a formal dissolution. Lübeck, Hamburg and Bremen continued to act together, and Bergen’s
institutional records and office-holders followed their own chronology. [Evidence:
`hse-claim-1669-conventional-terminus`](#hse-claim-1669-conventional-terminus). The essay ends at a
documented threshold, not at the disappearance of every Hanseatic practice.

What remains after the network is removed? Cities, first. Lübeck and Hamburg face their waterways;
Bruges remembers an emporium; London contains the Steelyard; Bergen’s harbour fabric outlives the
general Diet; Novgorod preserves the memory of Peterhof through a copied rule text. None is a
fragment of a missing national territory. Each is a political place in its own right, connected to
others through selective institutions.

Routes remain too, but as claims with limits. The seven published corridors do not recover ship
tracks. They connect waypoints at the level supported by the project’s evidence and disclose where
the reconstruction is generalized. Their commodity joins show that the network changes when the
question changes. Their dates are bounded by selected evidence, not by an imagined continuous
membership.

Documents remain most powerfully. A treaty can alter access across a sea. An embargo can attempt to
close a market and be evaded. A charter can assign building and staple rights. An ordinance can
organise elections and jurisdiction. A court book can carry an institution past the conventional
end of its wider assembly. These texts are not illustrations of a map hidden elsewhere. Together
they are the map of operative relations.

The comparison with Venice clarifies the argument by resisting symmetry. Venice was a republic
that held territories, governed possessions, organised convoys and maintained foreign quarters.
The Hansa was a looser association of merchants and autonomous towns whose collective reach rested
in privileges, meetings and overseas communities. Both can be studied through routes, commodities,
ports and documentary evidence. They must remain separate data sources because similar commercial
forms operated within different political structures.

<VeniceHanseaticComparison />

The point is not that Venice was territorial and the Hansa was immaterial. Venetian power also
depended on negotiation, and Hanseatic power produced substantial buildings, archives and coercive
effects. The comparison asks a narrower question: what kinds of political claim become visible when
we switch among territory, route, foreign quarter, commodity, authority and evidence? A single
colour wash answers only one of those questions and answers the Hanseatic case particularly badly.

A sea between cities is not empty space. It is filled by voyages, seasons, dangers, credit,
translation, storage, tolls, privileges and expectations. Yet those relations do not necessarily
compose a sovereign body. The Hansa’s historical importance lies partly in its capacity to sustain
continental-scale cooperation without acquiring the shape modern cartography most readily
recognises.

That is why the League left no map adequate to the name we give it. The cartographic record is not
silent: it shows worlds, seas and cities with extraordinary force. The institutional record is not
silent either: it preserves rules, charters, treaties, recesses and court books. The silence appears
only when we demand that one medium display every relation at once.

The answer is not to invent the missing territory. It is to keep the sources in productive tension.
Show the city, then ask what joins it to another. Draw the corridor, then state whether it is a
documented connection or a generalized reconstruction. Name the privilege, then name the host
authority. Mark 1669, then follow Bergen to 1764. Let the network appear - and let it disappear - so
that the evidence beneath the line remains visible.

</Section>
