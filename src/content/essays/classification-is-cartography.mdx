# Classification Is Cartography

## The border inside the catalogue

A map assigns things to positions. A classification assigns them to categories.
Both create neighbourhoods, boundaries, centres and margins. An object placed
under “nautical chart” becomes comparable with some sheets and distant from
others. A place recorded under one authorised name becomes findable through
that name and harder to retrieve through another. The catalogue is a map of the
collection.

Classification is necessary. Without shared fields, a corpus cannot be searched,
filtered, counted or linked. The danger is not that categories are artificial.
It is that their construction becomes invisible while their effects remain.

This essay treats taxonomy as spatial work. It asks where the system draws its
internal borders, which objects are forced to choose one side, and how
uncertainty can remain queryable instead of being cleaned away.

<Section id="category-draws-a-border" title="A category draws a border" rooms={['theatre', 'border']}>

Suppose a catalogue offers two types: “map” and “diagram.” A medieval T–O image,
transit schematic or cosmological chart may fit both. Requiring one value turns
an analytic distinction into a border. Statistics downstream will then report
two clean populations produced partly by the database rule.

Multi-value fields soften the boundary but do not remove it. The available
terms still define the universe of description. “Other” collects disagreement
without explaining it. Free text preserves nuance but may fragment retrieval.
Every schema trades some expressive range for shared use.

The first discipline is to state the purpose of a category. Medium, function,
genre, depicted subject and institutional collection are different axes. A
portolan chart can be parchment by medium, navigational by use, nautical by
genre and Mediterranean by coverage. Collapsing those axes into one “type”
produces a classification that cannot explain itself.

</Section>

<Section id="catalogue-makes-a-world" title="The catalogue makes a world" rooms={['archive', 'theatre']}>

Early modern cabinets, library catalogues, natural histories and atlases all
staged knowledge through ordered collections. Their sequence told a reader
which differences mattered. Modern standards make exchange possible on a far
larger scale, but they inherit the same responsibility.

The timeline marks influential systems that organised unlike domains. It is
not a genealogy in which one universal standard emerges as the destination.

A catalogue record often outlives the arrangement that created it. Historical
terms persist in subject headings. Colonial place names remain access points.
Maker attributions harden through copying. Digital migration can carry these
fields forward because preserving search appears safer than revisiting meaning.

The archive therefore contains two histories: the object and the description
of the object. Versioning a record makes the second history visible.

</Section>

<Section id="coordinate-systems-for-things" title="Coordinate systems for things" rooms={['map', 'theatre']}>

Controlled vocabularies resemble coordinate reference systems. They let
different records occupy a shared descriptive space. Crosswalks translate
between systems, but translation is rarely one-to-one. A local term may be
broader, narrower or conceptually different from the target.

Terra Chartarum's six-dimensional meta-lens is an example. Native essay axes are
not erased; they are mapped additively onto Measure, Witness, Use, Cosmos, Power
and Silence. The crosswalk enables discovery while retaining the original
vocabulary. It would become coercive if the six dimensions replaced every
essay's terms or claimed that unlike scores were naturally commensurable.

Place names reveal the same structure. A stable identifier can link variant
names without declaring one timeless label. Coordinates support spatial
matching, but historical places may have uncertain extent or move in relation
to rivers and coastlines. Identity is not reducible to a point.

Crosswalks should therefore publish direction and loss. Which source terms were
combined? Which distinctions disappeared? Can a user return to the native
record? Translation without that route becomes assimilation.

</Section>

<Section id="uncertainty-needs-a-field" title="Uncertainty needs a field" rooms={['archive', 'map']}>

Data cleaning often treats uncertainty as a defect. Dates become single years,
makers become names, places become points and ambiguous types become preferred
terms. The record passes validation while the evidence becomes less honest.

Uncertainty needs structure. A date can carry an interval and basis: printed,
catalogued, inferred from state, or approximate. Attribution can distinguish
signed, documented, traditional and disputed. Geometry can be observed,
reconstructed or schematic. Confidence should resolve to evidence, not float
as an unexplained percentage.

Null also needs meaning. “Unknown,” “not applicable,” “not recorded,”
“withheld” and “not yet researched” support different conclusions. One empty
cell cannot safely represent them all.

Structured uncertainty improves search. A user can ask for disputed
attributions or maps dated only by century. The alternative—hiding doubt in a
notes field—makes the catalogue look cleaner while making its uncertainty
impossible to query.

</Section>

<Section id="classify-with-escape-routes" title="Classify with escape routes" rooms={['theatre', 'archive', 'map']}>

A humane classification has escape routes. Records can hold multiple terms,
retain source language, link broader and narrower concepts, and record who
applied a description. Users can search synonyms without pretending they are
identical. Communities represented by the collection can contest harmful or
obsolete terms without erasing the history of prior cataloguing.

Interfaces should reveal filters as arguments. A facet count is not a fact
about the world; it is a fact about records matching a field under current
definitions. Tooltips, data dictionaries and visible zero states help readers
understand the boundary.

Machine classification raises the same questions at speed. Embeddings can
create useful neighbourhoods, but similarity depends on training data and
features. An automated label needs provenance, review status and a path for
correction. Confidence is not consent.

Classification is cartography because it creates a navigable world of things.
The ethical catalogue does not refuse to draw. It keeps the legend close,
preserves the source landscape and lets readers inspect the borders inside the
system.

</Section>
