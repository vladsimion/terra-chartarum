# Projection and Perspective

## Every view has a position

The most common lesson about map projections is also the least helpful: every
map is distorted. True, but incomplete. A flat map cannot preserve every
distance, direction, area and shape on a curved Earth. The useful question is
not how to escape distortion. It is which relations a projection chooses to
preserve, where its errors concentrate, and whether those choices fit the task.

Projection turns geometry into a contract. A conformal map preserves local
angles at the cost of area. An equal-area map preserves relative surface at the
cost of local shape. An equidistant map keeps selected distances, not all
distances everywhere. An azimuthal map can preserve direction from a chosen
centre. Each makes a promise with conditions.

Perspective adds another layer. Maps often imply a view from nowhere, but every
world image has an orientation, centre, extent and scale. Even a globe must be
seen from a position. The view is not a flaw; the hidden view is.

<Section id="flattening-is-a-contract" title="Flattening is a contract" rooms={['map', 'earth']}>

Imagine cutting an orange peel and pressing it flat. Tears and overlaps make
the problem visible. Mathematical projection replaces the torn peel with a
systematic transformation. The result is more predictable, not lossless.

Ptolemy's _Geography_ described methods for representing the inhabited world
with a graticule. Renaissance printed editions made those instructions newly
repeatable. Later projection families formalised different constraints as
navigation, surveying, thematic mapping and global comparison asked different
questions.

The chronology is not a march from primitive to correct. Innovations remain
useful because tasks differ.

The map reader should therefore look for the scope of the promise. “Preserves
area” means a thematic comparison of regions can be proportional; it does not
mean coastlines retain familiar shapes. “Preserves angles” supports local
bearing relations; it does not make a world map a transparent picture of
continental size.

</Section>

<Section id="mercator-keeps-a-bearing" title="Mercator keeps a bearing" rooms={['road', 'map']}>

Gerardus Mercator's 1569 projection makes rhumb lines-courses of constant
compass bearing-straight. That property gave the map a powerful navigational
logic. A mariner could plot a bearing as a line on the chart, though real
navigation still depended on instruments, observation, corrections and local
knowledge.

At world scale, Mercator greatly enlarges high latitudes. The familiar comparison
between Greenland and Africa exposes this area distortion: their apparent sizes
approach one another on many Mercator maps even though Africa is vastly larger.
The observation is important, but the moral is often simplified into “Mercator
is bad.”

A projection suited to one task can be irresponsible for another. Straight
rhumb lines do not justify using Mercator for a population cartogram or a
classroom comparison of continental area. Conversely, area distortion does not
erase its mathematical purpose. Critique becomes stronger when it connects a
property to a use.

Web maps revived a spherical form of Mercator because it supports a convenient
square, tiled, multi-zoom system. At neighbourhood scale its local angle
behaviour is useful; at a zoomed-out world view its area politics return.
Software can switch projections or disclose distortion. Familiar infrastructure
is a choice, not an alibi.

</Section>

<Section id="equal-area-changes-question" title="Equal area changes the question" rooms={['earth', 'border']}>

Equal-area projections change what comparison feels natural. Regions occupy
surface in correct proportion to one another, making maps of land cover,
population density or ecological change less likely to grant visual weight
through projection inflation.

But area fidelity does not settle political interpretation. A choropleth still
uses chosen boundaries. Large, sparsely populated units can dominate the page.
A national average can conceal local variation. Colour classes can exaggerate
thresholds. Projection solves one geometric problem while leaving statistical
and administrative choices intact.

The Peters controversy made these questions public in the late twentieth
century. Arno Peters promoted an equal-area projection as a corrective to
Eurocentric world maps, though the underlying cylindrical equal-area family
predated his version. The debate mattered because it revealed that projection
choice carries cultural consequence. It also showed how easily a technical
property can be marketed as a complete ethical solution.

There is no socially neutral geometry, but geometry is not reducible to
ideology. The ethical practice is to name both: the measurable property and the
institutional use.

</Section>

<Section id="perspective-has-a-viewer" title="Perspective has a viewer" rooms={['theatre', 'city']}>

Projection is usually described as a transformation of the Earth. Perspective
asks where the viewer stands. A bird's-eye city view combines elevation,
oblique sight and selective enlargement so streets and monuments can be seen
together. It may resemble a view from the air while exceeding what any single
eye could observe.

The viewpoint produces authority. From above, parcels align, routes connect and
crowds become patterns. The city appears available to inspection. Yet oblique
views can also foreground civic identity, giving towers, walls and sacred
buildings more space than strict geometry would allow.

Satellite imagery seems to complete the view from above, but it too is assembled:
sensor bands, orbital paths, rectification, mosaicking, dates and cloud removal
shape the image. “Perspective from space” is still a production pipeline.

The Theatre makes this visible. A map stages a world for a viewer. North-up,
centred longitude, crop and zoom decide where the audience is placed. Changing
the centre does not merely move objects; it interrupts the habit by which one
view becomes the world.

</Section>

<Section id="choose-and-disclose" title="Choose and disclose" rooms={['map', 'theatre']}>

Good projection practice begins with a sentence: this map needs to preserve
_what_, for _whom_, at _which scale_? Navigation, cadastral measurement,
continental comparison and polar climate analysis will produce different
answers.

The next sentence names sacrifice. A conformal web map should warn against
world-area comparison. An equal-area map should prepare readers for unfamiliar
shapes. A centred azimuthal view should identify its privileged point. A local
projection should publish its valid extent.

Interfaces can turn disclosure into exploration. A projection switch should not
offer a decorative carousel of globes. It should state what changes: area,
angle, distance, direction, interruption and centre. Reduced-motion and static
readers need the same explanation in prose.

The goal is not a projection without perspective. It is a map honest enough to
show its position. Distortion becomes informative when a reader can connect the
shape on the page to the contract that produced it.

</Section>
