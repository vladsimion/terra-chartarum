# Invisible Maps of Trade

## The sea that looks ready

The first impression of the Carta Pisana is not age but readiness. The parchment is
already busy. Lines spring from compass centres, cross one another, and turn the
Mediterranean into a field of possible movement. Place-names stand perpendicular to
the shore as if the coast were speaking outward. Islands are enlarged just enough to
be found. Inland Europe and Africa recede into comparative quiet. Nothing in the
picture waits for a modern road atlas to teach it how to connect one place to another.

That readiness is the chart’s achievement, and also its argument.

A portolan does not show trade in the way a ledger records a cargo or a customs roll
records a payment. It makes trade’s geography available to the eye. The sea becomes a
surface on which departure, call and arrival can be imagined together. A reader can
hold Majorca, Sicily, Crete and Alexandria in one frame and understand that their
relation is not adjacency on land but sequence across water. The coast is dense
because it is useful; the interior is quiet because it does not answer the question
the chart was built to ask.

It is tempting to call that blankness ignorance. Doing so mistakes a selective
instrument for a failed encyclopaedia. The chart is not trying to tell us everything
about the societies around the Mediterranean. It is trying to keep a vessel oriented
among named edges. Tony Campbell’s account of the portolan tradition treats the
combination of coastal names, compass organisation and repeated outline as a coherent
technical form, not an incomplete rehearsal for the modern topographic map. The sheet
succeeds by refusing most of the world.

But refusal has consequences. Once the coast has been reduced to calls and the water
to a field of headings, the network begins to look self-supporting. The line appears
to join two ports because geometry permits it. The names look equivalent because they
share a lettering system. A harbour under direct Venetian government, a feudatory
island, a foreign fondaco and a negotiated quarter can all sit on the same coast in
the same red ink. Their legal differences disappear at precisely the moment their
commercial relation becomes clearest.

This essay begins with that exchange. The portolan gives us movement and takes away
the machinery that sustains it. Terra Chartarum’s Venetian Maritime Network gives the
machinery back-not as a claim to reconstruct every voyage, but as a way to put dated
authority, ordered calls and controlled commodity tags beside the historical image.
The result is not a correction of the chart. It is an interrogation of what the
chart’s clarity costs.

<Section id="coast-as-infrastructure" title="The coast as infrastructure" rooms={['road', 'border']}>

Compare the Carta Pisana with the anonymous fourteenth-century chart held by the
Library of Congress. Their outlines are not identical, nor are their surviving
colours, annotations or emphases. Yet they share a way of making the sea legible. The
coast is a chain of names. The rhumb network offers a disciplined visual field.
Promontories and islands punctuate a surface whose practical meaning lies in passage.

The shore is therefore more than a boundary between blue and brown. It is
infrastructure.

Infrastructure is usually imagined as something built: mole, arsenal, warehouse,
beacon, galley, road. On a portolan, part of that infrastructure is editorial. A
place-name must be selected, repeated and placed where it can be read from the sea.
A coastline must be stabilised through copying and correction. A crowded cluster
must still allow the navigator to distinguish one call from the next. The chart turns
many local edges into one continuous interface.

That continuity is powerful because Mediterranean coasts were anything but
politically continuous. The VMN authority table uses twelve status terms across its
published port phases. Some rows describe the metropole or a capital; others a
subject port, colony, quarter, fondaco, tributary, feudatory or foreign port. Those
words are not ornamental labels. They distinguish direct rule from privilege,
possession from access, sovereignty from commercial presence. To flatten them all
into “Venetian colonies” would be to replace a negotiated maritime state with a
single colour wash.

The portolan performs a different flattening. It does not tell us that every harbour
is politically the same. It simply has no need to say how they differ. Its users need
the coast to continue past changes in ruler. A Genoese port can be essential to a
Venetian itinerary. A Byzantine capital can contain a Venetian quarter. A Mamluk
harbour can terminate a state convoy. The route depends on difference while the image
temporarily suspends it.

This is why the chart’s silences cannot be sorted into a simple list of errors.
Omitting the inland tax district is appropriate to a marine chart. Omitting the
jurisdiction of the quay may be equally appropriate to its immediate navigational
purpose. Yet when a later viewer treats the chart as a complete picture of trade, the
appropriate omission becomes a historical distortion. We see connected ports and
forget the customs officers, interpreters, consuls, warehouse keepers, pilots,
creditors, guards, sailors and coerced workers who made connection repeatable.

The coast looks ready because countless people kept making it ready.

<Section id="route-before-territory" title="A route before a territory" rooms={['border', 'road']}>

Pietro Vesconte’s signed chart of 1311 offers a useful resistance to the political
map. It gives the eastern Mediterranean as a set of coasts that can be followed and
crossed. His western Mediterranean atlas material extends the same logic across a
different field. Read together, the leaves suggest that a maritime network can be
spatially coherent without forming one continuous territory.

Venice’s power was often like this. It did hold land, islands, fortresses and cities,
sometimes for centuries. It also operated through arrangements that do not fit a
modern national silhouette. Monique O’Connell’s work on Venice’s maritime state
emphasises power and negotiation rather than a frictionless command radiating from
the lagoon. Frederic Lane’s maritime republic was an organised political economy of
ships, institutions and interests, not merely an unusually aquatic country.

The difference matters when we draw the network. A simple line from Venice to
Alexandria can imply one of two stories. In the first, geography supplies a corridor
and ships traverse it. In the second, the corridor exists only when a sequence of
relationships can be renewed: access to a harbour, protection at a vulnerable turn,
storage and brokerage at a terminus, credit in one city, information from another.
The geometric line is the residue of those arrangements, not their cause.

The published VMN routes are deliberately schematic for that reason. They join
authored waypoints in a stable order and avoid land at the resolution of the pinned
coastline. They are not recovered tracks. They do not claim a captain passed through
each displayed coordinate or that winds imposed the same curve in every season. A
route line is a reading aid: a statement that these named calls formed an intelligible
sequence in the project’s source model.

The distinction is easy to lose in an interactive map. Digital lines are crisp. They
can be highlighted, filtered and animated. Precision of display can masquerade as
precision of evidence. The responsible use of the layer therefore keeps its model
visible. Ports carry dated authority rows. Waypoints resolve to stable port IDs.
Operating windows are fields, not atmospheric reconstructions. Commodity terms come
from a controlled table, not from the thickness or speed of an animated stroke.

Once those constraints are visible, the digital layer can do something the portolan
cannot. Move the Atlas through time and the apparent permanence of the network
breaks. A port changes authority. A quarter closes or is reconstituted. A fortress
falls. A direct possession and a privileged foreign call no longer share the same
political present. The network persists, contracts or reroutes through discontinuous
rows.

The route comes before territory in the image. In history, route and territory keep
remaking one another.

<Section id="lagoon-ledger" title="The lagoon and the ledger" rooms={['city', 'road']}>

Jacopo de’ Barbari’s immense _View of Venice_ seems at first to offer the opposite of
a portolan. Where the marine chart strips the city to a coastal name, de’ Barbari
gives roofs, canals, churches, ships, yards and the improbable material density of the
lagoon. The city becomes an object that can be inspected. Its form floats whole before
us.

Yet the view and the portolan share a habit of selection. De’ Barbari makes Venice
appear self-contained at the moment it was least understandable in isolation. The
city’s stones depended on distant timber and labour; its markets on cargoes arriving
through layered privileges; its Arsenal on organised supplies, skills and state
direction. The lagoon concentrates a network whose spatial extent the print cannot
hold.

Andrea Bianco’s 1436 atlas provides another Venetian answer. Its portolan leaves,
tables and cosmographic material bind several modes of knowing into one manuscript.
The navigator’s coast belongs beside calculations and a world-picture. The atlas does
not simply zoom out from the lagoon. It collects practices by which a trading city
could orient itself at several scales.

To call Venice the centre of this system is correct only if “centre” is not confused
with “source”. The city concentrated decisions, finance, shipbuilding and
administration, but the knowledge that reached it came through pilots, merchants,
envoys, captives, clerks and travellers. Fra Mauro’s later world map makes this
dependence explicit in its abundance. Reports gathered in Venice become inscriptions
about places far beyond Venetian possession. The world is collected before it is
controlled.

The ledger offers a useful analogy. Double-entry does not make every transaction
Venetian; it makes dispersed obligations commensurable within a Venetian account.
Likewise the chart does not make every port Venetian. It makes heterogeneous ports
readable within a route. The achievement lies in relation, but so does the danger.
Once entries share a page, they can look as though they belong to one owner.

The authority vocabulary is a defence against that illusion. Venice is recorded as
metropole and capital in overlapping roles because a single status would suppress an
important distinction. Candia can be capital within Crete without becoming a second
metropole. A Duchy port can be feudatory rather than a directly governed subject.
Constantinople’s Venetian presence can be a quarter rather than a colony. Alexandria
can be indispensable while remaining foreign.

These are small words with large historiographical effects. They stop the network
from hardening into an empire-shaped diagram. They remind us that an invisible map of
trade is also a map of permissions.

</Section>

<Section id="convoy-calendar" title="The convoy calendar" rooms={['road', 'archive']}>

Lines alone do not make a route. Repetition does.

The state convoy-the _muda_-turned a possible voyage into an institution. Ships were
prepared, cargoes assembled, departures coordinated and calls ordered within
operating windows. Lane’s histories of Venice and Venetian shipbuilding make clear
how much organisation sat beneath maritime movement: hull form, labour, capital,
regulation and the seasonal problem of getting a valuable fleet out and home again.

A portolan is well suited to the spatial half of this institution. It keeps many
calls present at once. It does not, by itself, reveal the calendar that separates a
viable sequence from a decorative polyline.

Petrus Roselli’s 1466 Mediterranean chart and Battista Agnese’s sixteenth-century
atlas belong to a mature tradition in which the nautical image could be both working
knowledge and prestige object. Agnese’s finely made atlases, produced in Venice for
elite patrons, complicate any clean opposition between practical chart and symbolic
world-picture. A map can carry useful coastal structure and advertise the reach of
its owner’s knowledge. Its luxury does not erase its network logic; it changes the
social setting in which that logic is displayed.

An adaptive timeline beside these sheets makes a simple point. Routes have windows.
Ports have phases. Maps have dates. These temporalities overlap without coinciding.
A chart made in 1466 may preserve inherited coastal conventions. A route operating
across a period may encounter a changed authority at one waypoint. A modern dataset
may encode its interval with year fields even when the underlying historical source
speaks in a different granularity.

The interface should not smooth those differences away. It should let the reader see
that “the route” is a family of repeated, governed passages. The line stays visually
stable so that change elsewhere becomes legible: calls appear or disappear, statuses
alter, a selected year activates one set of rows rather than another. Stability is a
graphic control, not a historical assertion.

This distinction also protects us from false environmental confidence. The project’s
source hunt found modern wind, wave and current products and millennium-scale model
experiments, but no licensable dataset that could serve as an evidence-grade
reconstruction of medieval sailing costs. The essay therefore does not animate winds
or calculate a plausible number of days from a twentieth-century reanalysis. A
calendar can be shown where it is authored; a climate surface cannot be smuggled in
as history because it looks persuasive.

The convoy is invisible on the portolan not because time is unimportant, but because
the chart delegates time to other practices. The map waits. The institution moves.

### Commodities without arrows

Trade maps often begin with arrows and end with inevitability. Pepper flows west.
Timber flows south. Grain moves towards a hungry city. The thicker the arrow, the more
natural the movement appears.

The VMN commodity explorer takes a narrower approach. It uses a controlled set of
tags associated with authored routes. Selecting a commodity highlights matching
segments; it does not estimate tonnage, price, frequency or profit. That restraint is
important. A category can reveal structure without pretending to be a statistical
series.

Consider salt, grain, timber, wine, spices, textiles, metals, slaves and alum as words
in a maritime vocabulary. Each invokes a different geography of extraction, demand,
storage, risk and regulation. Yet on the portolan none receives an arrow. The chart
offers the coast on which their movement can be planned, not a thematic account of
what is moving.

This absence has two effects. First, it keeps the chart reusable. A coastline and set
of calls can support changing cargoes. Second, it makes the network appear
content-neutral. The same line seems available to any object and any traveller. In
practice, cargo changes the politics of passage. Bulk grain and compact spices demand
different calculations. Timber implicates shipbuilding and forest control. Enslaved
people expose violence that the neutral language of “commodity” can itself conceal.

An interactive tag must therefore be read as an index into the project’s model, not
as a moral equivalence. The category describes how a record is associated; it does
not settle what kind of historical relation the cargo represents. Highlighting a
route is the beginning of inquiry.

This is where the network view earns its place beside the map. A force-directed
diagram discards geographic distance and pulls ports together through shared route
membership. Hover a waypoint and its connections become visible. Select a commodity
and the diagram shows which authored routes carry that tag. The distortion is
deliberate. Where the portolan privileges coastal position, the network privileges
topology.

Neither is the true shape of trade. Their disagreement is useful. A distant port can
be structurally close; neighbouring harbours can belong to different circuits. The
reader learns to ask what each diagram preserves and what it sacrifices.

<Section id="world-collected" title="A world collected in Venice" rooms={['theatre', 'road']}>

Fra Mauro’s world map expands the argument beyond the Mediterranean without leaving
the logic of collection. The map is crowded with text and places, turned south-up,
and built from forms of report that exceed direct observation. It is not a portolan
enlarged to planetary scale, yet it carries the epistemic confidence of a city able
to gather distant testimony.

That confidence did not require Venice to possess the world it described. Knowledge
arrived through routes that crossed rival jurisdictions. A commercial republic could
be provincial in territory and cosmopolitan in information. The map makes this
asymmetry visible: a world assembled in one place from relations reaching far beyond
it.

Battista Agnese’s atlases later join portolan maps to cosmographic diagrams and newly
expanded oceanic geographies. Their makers worked within a market for objects that
could orient, instruct and impress. The network had become a cultural form. To own an
atlas was to possess a disciplined picture of connection even when one possessed
none of the connected shores.

This is the seductive achievement of the invisible map. It turns access into shape.
If a report, cargo or ship can reach Venice, the place it came from can be fitted into
a Venetian field of knowledge. The field is real, but it is not innocent. Routes
determine whose testimony arrives. Institutions decide what is copied. Patrons
determine which world-picture is worth making durable.

Harley’s insistence that maps be read as social documents helps us avoid choosing
between admiration and accusation. The Fra Mauro map can be an extraordinary
intellectual artefact and a selective product of its setting. A portolan can be an
effective navigational technology and a diagram whose clarity hides labour and
jurisdiction. Critical reading does not diminish the object. It restores the
conditions that made the object possible.

</Section>

<Section id="invisible-below-route" title="What disappears below the route" rooms={['border', 'road', 'city']}>

Return to the Carta Pisana after passing through the authority table, timeline and
network. The image has not changed. The same names line the coast. The same rhumbs
cross the sea. What changes is the question.

At first we asked where a ship might go. Now we ask what must remain in place for the
line to look effortless.

A ship must be built and supplied. A crew must be recruited, paid, disciplined and
fed. Information must travel ahead of cargo. Credit must bridge the interval between
departure and sale. A foreign ruler must tolerate or formalise a presence. A quarter
must be defended or renegotiated. A feudatory relationship must keep producing
access. A subject port must be administered. A convoy must leave within a workable
window. Violence must be threatened, enacted or remembered. None of these conditions
is guaranteed by the shortest line between two points.

The portolan’s visual language brackets those conditions because its purpose lies
elsewhere. The danger begins when we inherit the bracket as an explanation. Modern
network graphics can intensify the problem: smooth animation and exact coordinates
make connection look automatic. The answer is not to abandon abstraction. It is to
make the abstraction answerable to other records.

Terra Chartarum’s Atlas lets one port open into dated authority phases. The standalone
network lets one waypoint reveal connected routes. Commodity filters expose a
controlled thematic layer without inventing quantities. Deep links keep essay and
map in a shared state. Each device adds friction to the elegant line.

That friction is historical knowledge.

Trade is “invisible” here in two senses. Much of its supporting apparatus is absent
from the historical chart. Much of the network itself is also intangible: privilege,
credit, expectation, schedule, reputation and fear. Yet invisible does not mean
unmappable. It means that no single map should be allowed to close the account.

The portolan maps a coast. The authority table maps a vocabulary of power. The route
layer maps an authored sequence. The commodity explorer maps associations. The essay
maps the disagreements between them.

Together they replace the fantasy of frictionless flow with a more demanding image:
trade as a route continually produced by people who did not share one status, one
interest or one freedom of movement. The lines remain beautiful. They are no longer
innocent.

</Section>
