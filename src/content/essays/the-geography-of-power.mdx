# The Geography of Power

## Authority at street level

Maps of political power often begin too far away. Capitals receive stars,
states receive colours, borders receive thick lines. The image locates formal
authority, but it says little about how authority reaches a household. Power
arrives through an address, parcel, school district, utility connection, permit,
police beat, bus route, flood zone and queue. Its geography is infrastructural.

The city makes this distribution visible because many jurisdictions occupy the
same block. Property lines, service areas and electoral wards overlap. A person
may live inside a municipality yet outside a reliable water network; inside a
school catchment yet beyond a safe walking route; inside a voting district yet
outside the language in which its procedures are explained. The official line
and the lived reach of government are related but not identical.

This essay asks how maps make that gap legible without pretending that access
can be read directly from infrastructure alone.

<Section id="power-has-an-address" title="Power has an address" rooms={['city', 'border']}>

An address is a small administrative map. It places a dwelling inside a street
sequence, postal system, emergency response grid and property record. To have
an address is to become findable by services, markets and enforcement. To lack
one can mean practical exclusion even when a settlement is physically close.

House numbering and street naming appear mundane because their political work
has become routine. They let officials enumerate residents, issue notices,
collect fees and coordinate visits. The same legibility helps people claim
services and organise. Power is not contained in the number; it is enabled by
the systems that agree to recognise it.

Historical city plans show this transition unevenly. Giambattista Nolli's 1748
Rome distinguishes public circulation through streets, squares and selected
interiors. Nineteenth-century cadastral and insurance mapping disaggregates the
block into parcels, materials and risks. Twentieth-century zoning adds permitted
uses. Contemporary open data publishes service boundaries as queryable layers.
Each system answers a different administrative question.

<Section id="parcel-makes-a-subject" title="The parcel makes a subject" rooms={['city', 'map']}>

The cadastral parcel translates occupation into a bounded object that can be
owned, assessed, transferred and disputed. It joins geometry to a legal record.
Its apparent precision can conceal the negotiations, informal uses and shared
rights that do not fit the polygon.

Parcel mapping produces a particular urban subject: the registered owner,
tenant, taxpayer or liable party attached to an address. People who live or
work outside those categories may become hard to see. Informal extensions,
collective tenure and seasonal occupation can appear as error against the
official base.

This does not make cadastral data dispensable. Clear records can protect claims
and expose unequal concentration. The task is to keep the map's category visible.
A parcel layer shows recognised property units; it does not show every social
relation to the land.

Overlaying eviction filings, code enforcement or tax arrears on parcels can
reveal administrative pressure. Yet such maps also risk turning households into
problem symbols. Ethical design should aggregate where possible, explain the
recording process and avoid making vulnerability easier to target.

</Section>

<Section id="service-area-is-political" title="A service area is political" rooms={['city', 'earth']}>

Infrastructure makes jurisdiction tangible. Water pressure, sewer connections,
electric reliability, tree canopy, clinics and frequent transit distribute
health and time. Their service areas rarely end at one obvious boundary. They
follow engineering capacity, historical investment, topography, contracts and
political choice.

A map of pipes can show where a network exists. It cannot by itself show whether
water is affordable, safe or continuously supplied. A transit line does not
measure the reliability of the trip or the safety of the walk to the stop.
Availability is not access.

The distinction changes mapping practice. Instead of a binary service polygon,
an access map can combine travel time, schedule, cost, capacity and barriers,
while keeping each input inspectable. Uncertainty should expand where data is
old or coverage uneven.

Environmental systems add another scale. Flood zones and heat islands cross
administrative borders, but mitigation budgets and emergency response do not.
The geography of risk and the geography of responsibility can therefore diverge.
Placing both on the same map reveals where jurisdiction becomes an excuse for
inaction.

</Section>

<Section id="visibility-cuts-both-ways" title="Visibility cuts both ways" rooms={['map', 'archive']}>

Reformers have long used maps to expose hidden conditions. Charles Booth's
poverty maps, sanitation surveys and disease investigations turned urban
inequality into evidence that officials could not easily dismiss. Contemporary
community maps document missing crossings, pollution, displacement and police
encounters.

But visibility cuts both ways. Booth's categories judged households through an
observer's social hierarchy. Redlining maps converted racialised assessment into
an apparatus for disinvestment. Predictive systems can recycle biased records
as if the density of past enforcement measured the density of harm.

The same technical act—geocoding an address—can support accountability or
surveillance. Purpose, access and consequence decide the politics. A responsible
map should record who collected the data, under which definition, and which
people were exposed by its publication.

Absence also needs interpretation. A blank area in a complaints dataset may
signal satisfaction, or it may signal language barriers, distrust and lack of
access to reporting. Silence is not a zero.

</Section>

<Section id="map-power-from-below" title="Map power from below" rooms={['city', 'road']}>

Mapping power from below begins with a journey rather than a jurisdiction.
How long does it take to reach water, work, school, care or safety? Which gates,
fares, forms and informal checkpoints interrupt the route? Where does a right
exist on paper but fail in practice?

Participatory mapping can supply knowledge absent from administrative systems,
but participation is not a decorative workshop. Contributors need control over
what is recorded, who can see it and how it will be used. Sensitive paths may
need deliberate generalisation or omission.

The strongest civic map holds three layers apart: formal responsibility,
material provision and lived access. Agreement among them is evidence of a
system working. Disagreement is not noise to smooth away. It identifies the
geography in which power is negotiated.

Seen at street level, power loses the clean outline of the state map. It becomes
a field of doors that open, services that arrive, routes that fail and records
that recognise some people more readily than others. That geography is harder
to draw—and closer to how authority is lived.

</Section>
