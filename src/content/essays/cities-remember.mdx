# Cities Remember

## The map after the street

A city can forget in public. A gate is pulled down and its name remains on a road.
A convent closes and the plot is divided. A defensive wall becomes a promenade.
A stream is culverted, an alley widened, a district cleared for a station, a house
renumbered, a property line preserved behind a new facade. The present city absorbs
these changes so completely that the old order becomes difficult to see while
walking through it.

The map is often asked to repair that loss. Put an earlier plan beside a modern one,
we are told, and the buried city will reappear. The invitation is powerful because
maps make difference look inspectable. Two streets coincide; a vanished wall cuts
across a square; a church survives while the houses around it have changed. The map
seems to remember what the city has forgotten.

But a map does not remember like a witness with a complete view. It remembers
according to its material, purpose and institutional life. A marble cadastral plan
cuts selected architecture into stone. A printed prospect turns a city into a
spectacle. A surveyed ichnographic plan distinguishes forms of public access. A fire
insurance sheet classifies construction and hazard for underwriters. Each preserves
something by making other things peripheral or absent.

That selectivity is not a defect to be corrected by adding enough layers. It is the
historical subject. A city map carries forward an argument about which urban facts
can be stabilised: walls, monuments, parcels, routes, interiors, values, dangers.
Later readers inherit both the information and the original terms of attention.

This essay follows nine maps and map systems across that problem. Rome is the main
thread, from the surviving fragments of the Severan marble plan through Leonardo
Bufalini and Giambattista Nolli. Nuremberg, Venice, London and Paris show the city
performed as a whole. Buffalo’s Sanborn sheets reveal a different memory, organised
around combustible materials and commercial risk. Together they show why “the city”
on a map is never simply an area. It is an edited relationship between ground,
building, institution and future use.

<Section id="city-as-fragment" title="The city survives as a fragment" rooms={['archive', 'city']}>

The **Forma Urbis Romae** begins with an apparent promise of totality. Made in the
early third century, the enormous marble plan was mounted on a wall in the Temple of
Peace complex. The Stanford Digital Forma Urbis project describes a surface roughly
18.1 metres wide and 13 metres high, with architecture engraved at about 1:240.
Rooms, walls, columns and stairways were cut at a scale capable of making the built
city extraordinarily specific.

The plan’s survival reverses that scale. It is known through 1,186 fragments rather
than one intact image. The pieces range from substantial assemblies to small engraved
remnants whose place in Rome is unknown. The city reaches us not as a miniature
whole but as a problem of joins, attributions and absences.

This matters because reconstruction is easily disguised by the confidence of a
diagram. A fragment physically joined to another fragment occupies a different
evidentiary state from one associated with a district because it depicts a
recognisable structure. A piece with a few walls but no secure location is different
again. If all three appear in the same line weight on a completed plan, the
reconstruction erases its own reasoning.

The ledger below does not republish Stanford’s protected project photography and
does not claim to replace its searchable record. It stages the editorial distinction
that this essay needs. “Joined” refers to material or securely continuous evidence.
“Proposed location” refers to an attribution that should remain contestable.
“Unplaced” refuses the visual temptation to fill the blank.

<FragmentLedger />

The blank is itself information. According to the project’s account, the marble plan
privileged architecture. Political and sacred boundaries were not drawn as a
separate thematic system. Even the Tiber is legible largely through the space left
between built edges. The city remembered in marble is therefore not the total city
of inhabitants, sounds, rights and movement. It is a city made durable as
architectural ground.

Later plans of Rome can appear to complete what the fragments lost. Bufalini’s 1551
plan and Nolli’s 1748 plan provide continuous printed surfaces, named places and a
recognisable relation to the modern city. Yet they do not restore the Severan map.
They record other Romes through other instruments and institutions. To place a
fragment beside Nolli is useful only when the gap of fifteen centuries remains
visible. The later plan can supply orientation; it cannot convert a proposed ancient
location into a physical join.

This is the first way a city map remembers: not by overcoming ruin, but by letting a
surviving mark carry an incomplete address. The archive becomes more truthful when
it distinguishes the strength of that address instead of drawing every fragment
home.

</Section>

<Section id="city-performs-itself" title="The city performs itself" rooms={['theatre', 'city']}>

A city portrait answers a different desire. Where the fragmented plan frustrates
the whole, the printed view offers the whole immediately. Walls enclose it. Towers
rise above it. Streets, roofs, markets and churches settle into one visual body. The
viewer stands outside, above or impossibly across from the city and sees more than a
single human position could reveal.

The 1493 Nuremberg view in the _Liber Chronicarum_ announces “Nuremberga” above a
concentrated walled settlement. Named churches and gates organise recognition.
Buildings crowd toward the right edge while an approach road leads the eye into a
defended civic body. The page does not behave like a modern measured plan. It makes
Nuremberg memorable through profile, enclosure and emblematic density.

Jacopo de’ Barbari’s _View of Venice_ of 1500 expands this performance to monumental
scale. Printed from six woodblocks, the lagoon city becomes a single inspectable
object surrounded by water, shipping and divine winds. Its immense detail promises
local knowledge, but the viewing position is constructed. No observer could occupy
the air above the lagoon and see every roof, canal and island in this way.

That impossibility is not evidence that the view is careless. It is how the image
does civic work. Venice appears self-contained yet connected to movement. Its
waterborne edges are clear; its buildings form a dense, controlled surface; Mercury
hovers over a commercial republic that can be apprehended as a whole. The view
remembers Venice as the city wished to become visible.

The comparison below brings Nuremberg’s book-page city beside one sheet of Nolli’s
surveyed Rome. The images differ in date, scale, crop and purpose, so the slider is
not a registration. It asks what kind of recognition each image produces. In the
chronicle view, towers and walls let the city perform its identity. In Nolli, a plan
reader enters a field of streets, blocks and accessible interiors.

The distinction becomes more consequential with Braun and Hogenberg’s _Civitates
Orbis Terrarum_, begun in 1572. Hundreds of plans and prospects place different
cities inside a repeatable atlas form, accompanied by descriptive texts. Local
identity becomes comparable. Dress, wall, skyline, street and setting can be read
across pages as elements of an urban genre.

Comparison preserves and flattens at once. It makes distant cities available to the
same reader, but it encourages each place to become a specimen of “the city.” The
atlas remembers a civic world through the regularity of its own sequence. The
individual image might insist on local monument or costume; the volume teaches the
reader to move from city to city.

This is why a historical view should not be treated merely as a less accurate plan.
Its truth lies partly in performance. It records what could be made to stand for the
city: enclosure, abundance, sacred prominence, commercial access, recognisable
profile. A wall on such an image is fortification, edge and emblem at the same time.

</Section>

<Section id="walls-after-walls" title="Walls after walls" rooms={['city', 'archive']}>

Walls are among the easiest urban memories to narrate. They draw a boundary, they
survive in place-names, and their removal often produces boulevards or ring roads
whose shape remains visible on later maps. Put two plans side by side and the story
appears clean: enclosure, demolition, expansion.

Historical maps complicate that sequence because a wall can outlive its military
function inside the image. It continues to define the city long after settlement has
grown beyond it. It can be copied from an older source, emphasised for civic identity
or retained because a publisher’s plate still carries the line. The mapped wall has
its own life, neither identical to standing masonry nor simply false.

Bufalini’s Rome of 1551 demonstrates the authority of a continuous printed plan at a
moment when ancient fabric, papal intervention and contemporary streets coexisted.
The Aurelian Walls supply a powerful perimeter, yet the city inside is uneven. Large
tracts within the ancient circuit were not densely built. The inherited enclosure
remembers an imperial scale that the sixteenth-century settlement did not uniformly
occupy.

The so-called Agas map of London creates a similarly compelling urban body. Its
woodcut tradition presents streets, buildings, river traffic and walls in a
bird’s-eye language. The University of Victoria’s Map of Early Modern London now
turns the image into a scholarly, hyperlinked environment. A historic view becomes
an index: a gate, parish or street can connect to textual records and later
knowledge.

The digital edition does more than reproduce memory. It reorganises it. Search,
links and scholarly entries make features available in ways the original printed
surface could not. The old wall remains visible, but it now participates in a new
system of addresses. That transformation is valuable precisely because it is
acknowledged as an edition rather than disguised as unmediated access to London.

Louis Bretez’s great perspective plan of Paris, commonly called the Turgot map,
offers another form of afterlife. Commissioned in the eighteenth century and issued
in twenty sectional plates with a key, it presents Paris from an oblique viewpoint
while allowing the city to be assembled as a grand whole. Buildings and gardens
rise; streets remain traceable; the surrounding edge stages an urban order for
inspection.

Across these maps, a boundary can remember several cities at once: the legal city,
the defended city, the built-up city and the city as a publisher or patron wishes it
seen. Demolition does not reduce those meanings to a single before-and-after line.
When a later boulevard follows a former wall, the continuity is spatial. When a
printed wall persists after material change, the continuity is representational.
Both are historical, but they are different claims.

The responsible map interface should therefore ask what has persisted. Is the
geometry derived from a survey or copied from another print? Does a wall line mark
standing fabric, jurisdiction, memory or graphic convention? “Still visible” is an
invitation to investigate, not an answer.

</Section>

<Section id="public-private-ground" title="Public and private ground" rooms={['city', 'map']}>

Nolli’s _Nuova Pianta di Roma_ is often celebrated for the way its figure-ground
language joins streets and certain accessible interiors into a continuous white
field. Churches, courtyards and other spaces that a reader could enter participate
in the public texture; private building mass is dark. The result makes urban access
legible without reducing it to the open air.

That visual grammar is a form of memory. A modern street map generally preserves the
route through Rome and labels major monuments, but it may not reproduce the same
distinction between accessible interior and private mass. Nolli records a social and
architectural relation that can disappear even where the building remains.

It would be easy to call the white field “public space” and treat it as a timeless
property. The term needs care. Access was conditioned by institution, custom, time,
status and use. A church interior was not the same political space as a street. The
plan’s graphic continuity is analytically fertile because it draws them together;
it should not make their differences vanish.

The overlay below places one Nolli sheet above an OpenStreetMap-derived image of
modern Rome. The opacity control gives the historical engraving enough instability
to be inspected rather than accepted. Move it toward zero to read the present map;
increase it to let Nolli’s linework dominate. Notice how quickly apparent
coincidence produces confidence.

The annotation behind the overlay follows the W3C/IIIF georeferencing form used by
Allmaps: a source image and mask are attached to geographic control points. Publishing
those points matters because “georeferenced” should not function as a magical label.
Another reader can inspect what was matched and reject the registration.

This particular registration is explicitly exploratory. It uses four approximate
visual correspondences on a cropped reproduction. It has no calculated,
evidence-grade residual. The modern image uses a web-map projection and the engraved
sheet contains its own distortions. A convincing local overlap can sit beside a
substantial error elsewhere.

The warning changes how the overlay should be read. If an old street and a modern
street appear to coincide, the interface has generated a hypothesis. Verification
requires a better georeferencing model, documented control points, residual analysis
and often other survey or cadastral evidence. If a church footprint seems stable,
the image still cannot establish uninterrupted access, fabric or ownership.

This does not make the overlay useless. It makes its use specific. The layer helps a
reader identify where to look and what kind of persistence might be at stake. It
returns Nolli’s public-private grammar to the present city as a question.

</Section>

<Section id="risk-remembers-block" title="Risk remembers the block" rooms={['archive', 'city']}>

The Sanborn fire insurance maps remember a city for a reader who may never visit it.
Their audience needed to judge risk: construction material, building use, water
supply, street width, openings, fire protection and the proximity of hazardous
activities. The sheets are exceptionally detailed because insurance required
detail, not because the map aspired to preserve every urban fact.

Buffalo’s 1899 volume belongs to that system. Across more than a hundred sheets, the
city becomes an inventory of blocks and structures. Colour, abbreviations and
symbols support comparison. A building is not only a shape but a bundle of
attributes relevant to loss. The map’s apparent intimacy with the block is
institutional: it sees through the priorities of underwriting.

That purpose produces a powerful afterlife. Historians, residents, planners and
environmental researchers use Sanborn maps to locate disappeared factories,
tenements, yards, tanks and services. The commercial classification built for one
future-the probability and cost of fire-becomes evidence for many later pasts.

The transfer is never neutral. What an insurer recorded in precise colour can seem
more historically real than what the sheet omitted. Household relations, informal
uses, racial exclusion, tenancy and daily movement may be essential to the life of
the block without occupying equivalent graphic space. The map remembers the
building as a risk object more readily than the building as a home.

Revision deepens the problem. Sanborn sheets could be updated through paste-on
corrections and later editions. A map that appears to describe one date might carry
material from several observations. The archive must distinguish edition, sheet and
revision rather than assign the entire image one stable birthday. In this respect,
the insurance map belongs beside <a href="/essays/maps-that-age/">Maps That Age</a>:
the physical carrier can become a record of repeated institutional attention.

The map is therefore neither an innocent snapshot nor only an instrument of power.
It is a highly structured witness. Its classifications can recover vanished urban
fabric with astonishing specificity, while the reasons for that specificity
constrain what the evidence means.

When a Sanborn block is overlaid on a present parcel map, the same discipline applies
as with Nolli. A footprint correspondence is not a complete social continuity. It
may establish that a structure occupied a location or that a use was recorded there.
It cannot by itself tell us whose memory survived, who was displaced, or which
unmapped practices gave the block its life.

</Section>

<Section id="overlay-is-argument" title="An overlay is an argument" rooms={['map', 'city', 'archive']}>

Every overlay chooses a common ground. Two images must be cropped, transformed and
ordered. One becomes the base; another becomes the layer. Control points declare
that selected marks refer to the same places. Opacity decides which image dominates.
Even a technically excellent registration is therefore an interpretation before it
is a discovery.

The strongest version of the tool makes those decisions inspectable. It identifies
the source images and rights. It publishes the annotation. It labels estimated or
uncalculated error. It distinguishes a visual correspondence from a historical
claim. It lets a reader reduce the historical layer instead of trapping the present
under it.

The weakest version offers wonder without friction: “then and now” perfectly fused,
with no account of projection, crop, survey, revision or control points. The result
can turn geometric resemblance into a myth of urban persistence. A street seems
ancient because two strokes meet. A boundary seems natural because it survives on
several maps. A demolished district becomes legible as empty space without the
violence or administration that produced the clearing.

The nine records in this essay suggest a different practice.

From the Forma Urbis, keep the evidence state attached to the fragment. Do not allow
a reconstructed whole to erase the distinction between physical join and proposed
location.

From Nuremberg, Venice and the _Civitates_, recognise the city portrait as a
performance. Perspective and enclosure are not failed survey; they are techniques
for making civic identity visible and comparable.

From Bufalini, the Agas tradition and Turgot, ask which boundary or monument the
printed city carries forward, and whether the persistence belongs to the ground, the
plate, the edition or the patron’s urban idea.

From Nolli, preserve the map’s analysis of access without converting white and black
into timeless public and private categories. Let georeferencing open a question,
then publish enough evidence for the registration to be challenged.

From Sanborn, use extraordinary block detail while retaining the commercial purpose
that selected it. A risk map can remember vanished industry and still remain silent
about much of urban life.

These are not reasons to distrust city maps. They are reasons to grant them
historical depth. A map does not merely store an earlier arrangement of streets. It
stores a way of observing, classifying and revising the city. When that map is
digitised, linked and overlaid, its way of seeing becomes actionable again.

The city remembers through fabric, names, habits and institutions. The map remembers
through material marks, graphic conventions, copies, repairs, catalogues and new
interfaces. Sometimes the two memories coincide. Sometimes the map preserves what
the street has lost. Sometimes it perpetuates an order the city has outgrown.

The task is not to make the past perfectly transparent over the present. It is to
show the seam: where an old claim has been fitted to new ground, where the match is
strong, where it fails, and what each layer was built to see.

</Section>
