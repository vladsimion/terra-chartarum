# Palimpsest Landscapes

## The ground keeps several times

Landscape is often called a palimpsest: a manuscript scraped and written over,
with earlier text still visible beneath the new. The metaphor is useful because
it resists the idea that one date owns the ground. It is dangerous because land
is not a passive page. Rivers move, soil accumulates, buildings are quarried,
roads are reused, memories attach, and later investigators decide which traces
to name.

A landscape does not preserve every period equally. Stone survives differently
from timber. A property boundary can outlast the institution that drew it. A
ruin may be reconstructed until the reconstruction becomes the most visible
layer. Absence can result from decay, deliberate demolition, later building or
the methods of the survey.

The palimpsest is therefore not a stack waiting to be peeled back to an original
truth. It is a set of interacting traces whose survival and interpretation both
require explanation.

<Section id="palimpsest-is-not-metaphor-alone" title="Palimpsest is not metaphor alone" rooms={['archive', 'earth']}>

Physical processes produce temporal depth. Floodplains bury surfaces; erosion
cuts through them. Crop marks reveal buried walls by changing plant growth.
Soil and moisture make former roads legible from the air. LiDAR can model subtle
relief beneath woodland. Geophysics detects contrasts without excavation.

Each technique sees a different property. Aerial photography records reflected
light at a moment. LiDAR estimates surfaces from returned pulses. Magnetometry
measures local magnetic variation. Excavation exposes stratigraphic relation
destructively and at limited extent. Combining them can strengthen an
interpretation, but no composite becomes a direct view through time.

The map should retain those methods as attributes. A line inferred from crop
marks is not identical to a standing wall surveyed on the ground. Rendering
both as the same solid stroke turns evidential difference into false
continuity.

</Section>

<Section id="trace-changes-with-scale" title="A trace changes with scale" rooms={['map', 'archive']}>

At field scale, a bank may be measurable. At regional scale, it becomes part of
a hypothesised route or boundary. Generalisation is necessary, but it changes
the claim. Joined fragments can look more certain than the observations from
which they were assembled.

Historical map regression-comparing dated maps and surveys-adds another kind of
trace. A road appearing on several editions may record persistence, or it may
show copying. A missing feature may have vanished from the ground, fallen
outside the map's purpose, or been omitted by revision.

Scale should be an interface control and a prose warning. Zooming out may hide
uncertain fragments; zooming in may reveal that an apparently continuous line
is a reconstruction. A good historical layer changes symbology with scale
instead of promising the same certainty everywhere.

</Section>

<Section id="city-reuses-ground" title="The city reuses its ground" rooms={['city', 'archive']}>

Cities make palimpsest visible through reuse. Walls become property lines.
Theatres become housing foundations. Buried watercourses influence streets and
basements. Demolished fortifications become boulevards or parks. The past does
not sit beneath the present in tidy layers; it is recruited into it.

Rome's Forma Urbis fragments preserve a Severan marble plan while the city they
depict has been rebuilt many times. Nolli's eighteenth-century plan records a
different relation between public interior and street. Modern mapping can align
fragments, plan and contemporary fabric, but the alignment is interpretive.
Different surveys, damage and uncertain fragment placement prevent perfect
registration.

Urban archaeology also reflects planning power. Development-led excavation
occurs where construction opens ground. Monumental centres receive different
attention from ordinary housing or industrial outskirts. A density map of
finds can become a density map of investigation.

The city is not more historical where the database has more points. The
database is one layer in the palimpsest.

</Section>

<Section id="reconstruction-is-argument" title="Reconstruction is an argument" rooms={['theatre', 'archive']}>

Reconstruction joins fragments into a possible whole. It can make a lost wall,
coastline or street network understandable. Its persuasive clarity is exactly
why it needs visible provenance.

A solid polygon suggests observed extent. A hatched one can mark inference; a
range can show competing positions. Dates should be intervals where evidence
supports only intervals. Alternate reconstructions should remain switchable
rather than averaged into a shape no scholar proposed.

Three-dimensional views increase the burden. Texture, light and complete roofs
can make a hypothesis feel witnessed. A reconstruction should distinguish
measured geometry, analogous completion and artistic staging. The viewer needs
to know which parts survive.

This is a theatrical problem in the best sense. Reconstruction stages evidence
so an absent landscape can be considered. The ethical aim is not to avoid the
stage. It is to keep the scenery from claiming to be the ruin.

</Section>

<Section id="keep-layers-answerable" title="Keep layers answerable" rooms={['archive', 'earth', 'map']}>

An answerable palimpsest layer carries a source, observation method, date of
observation, interpreted period, geometry status and confidence. “Confidence”
should not be a mysterious number; it should resolve to reasons such as
excavated, surveyed, remotely sensed, documented on a contemporary plan, or
inferred between fragments.

Comparisons need registration boundaries. A slider between two maps can reveal
change, but differences in projection, crop and survey precision must not be
read as changes in the ground. Control points and residual error belong beside
the visual.

Absence should remain selectable. A demolished structure, a dry channel and an
unlocated feature are three different states. Deleting all three from the
current layer reproduces the fantasy that only the visible present is real.

The landscape keeps several times, but not on its own. Materials survive,
institutions record, communities remember and investigators interpret. A
palimpsest map is strongest when it shows those acts separately enough that a
reader can ask how each line came to be there.

</Section>
