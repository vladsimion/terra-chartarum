# Nomen Errans

## A name is not a thing. It is a job, and the same word can be given several.

Dacia can name a Roman province north of the Danube. It can name a later province
south of it, a civil diocese, a figure on imperial coinage, the Scandinavian north
on a medieval world map, or a province restored by an antiquarian more than a
millennium after Roman administration withdrew. The letters remain remarkably
stable. The thing indicated by them does not.

That instability creates a familiar cartographic temptation. Put the uses in date
order, place them on one map, and the eye will invent a journey: the name appears
to flow from one territory into the next. But chronological adjacency is not
transmission, a shared spelling is not continuity, and a map that connects every
node would make a stronger historical claim than the surviving evidence allows.

This essay follows a stricter route. Each public career comes from a reviewed row
in the Corpus Nominum Daciae. Each row states who used the word, what it referred
to, when, on which source, at what locator and with what confidence. The map opens
only where a compiled Atlas layer honestly covers that referent. A line between
careers appears only when the relationship itself has passed review. An empty
space is therefore not unfinished design. It is the visible form of a claim the
evidence has not earned.

Read this beside <EssayLink slug="dacia">Terra Sigillata</EssayLink>, which asks
how maps and catalogues name the ground. _Nomen Errans_ asks the inverse question:
what happens when the name leaves that ground behind?

<Section id="premise" title="A word is not a territory" rooms={['border', 'archive']}>

Historical geography often begins with a proper noun and quietly treats it as a
stable key. A place record called Dacia seems to promise that every occurrence of
the word can be joined to the same object. That is convenient for a database and
dangerous for an argument.

The corpus separates three things that prose too easily collapses. The **lexical
form** is the written word. The **referent** is what a particular use makes the
word indicate. The **relationship** is an evidenced claim about how two uses are
connected. Repeating the first does not establish either of the other two.

This distinction matters even inside Roman administration. The Trajanic province,
the later Dacia south of the Danube and the _dioecesis Daciae_ are not three dates
for one stable polygon. They are different administrative objects. The coin legend
is another kind of use again: a mint makes the name circulate without supplying a
boundary. Ptolemy's coordinate catalogue, Eutropius's narrative, the _Notitia
Dignitatum_ and the imperial coin series answer different questions. The ledger
keeps their source families because their silences differ as much as their words.

The release page does not copy their chronology into prose. The panels below are
compiled from the same tables as the data release. Correcting a date or locator in
the corpus changes the essay on the next deterministic build; demoting a row below
review removes it from the public sequence. The text cannot preserve an older,
more confident version by accident.

</Section>

<Section id="careers" title="The careers of the word" rooms={['border', 'archive', 'map']}>

Each step is one reviewed use. The fate class names the operation performed on the
word: **applicatio** applies it to an administrative or representational referent;
**translatio** carries it to different ground; **restitutio** deliberately restores
an earlier name. These labels classify what the source does. They do not claim a
genealogy between sources.

<NameCareers caption="Reviewed uses of Dacia, compiled from the Corpus Nominum Daciae. Source, locator, confidence and Atlas coverage travel with every career; normalized rows remain counted but withheld." />

The medieval step is the fracture that makes the method necessary. On the Hereford
Mappa Mundi, around 1300, _Dacia_ belongs to the Scandinavian north. The source is
one membrane and therefore has no page or sheet number to pretend to precision;
the whole work is the locator. Its reviewed row says what the map calls the region.
It does not say that Denmark descends from the Roman province, that the name moved
there by one recoverable route, or that a medieval reader understood every earlier
career gathered on this page.

The early-modern return is different again. Ortelius's _Daciarum Moesiarumque
vetus descriptio_ belongs to the _Parergon_, the historical supplement to the
_Theatrum_. Its Dacia is an antiquarian reconstruction: an ancient province made
present on a printed sheet through a Renaissance reading of old authorities. The
fate class is therefore _restitutio_, not continuity. The map's date tells us when
Ortelius restored the province to view, not that the province had persisted until 1595.

The source apparatus beneath the panels is as important as the sequence above
them. A repository, citation and locator let a reader ask whether a row says only
what its witness can support. Confidence is spelled out rather than encoded by
colour. Careers without an honest Atlas layer say so instead of opening the nearest
available geometry and hoping proximity will be mistaken for evidence.

</Section>

<Section id="migration" title="A sequence without a line" rooms={['map', 'border', 'archive']}>

The diagram below makes the problem deliberately uncomfortable. It has reviewed
nodes, a chronological direction and no relationship lines. The corpus contains
candidate relationships - derivation, revival and explicit non-relationship - but none
has passed the separate human-review threshold required for drawing. The figure
does not convert their order into an arrow.

<NameMigration caption="Reviewed careers projected against their compiled Atlas coverage. A relationship line is emitted only after its own ledger row passes human review; chronology alone never draws one." />

Some careers can still open useful map states. The Trajanic province opens the
Roman sites and network at AD 150. The coin legend opens the province it names,
not a guessed findspot for an unspecified coin. Ortelius opens the research-tier
attestation that records the reconstruction. Every link carries the essay slug,
so the Atlas can return the reader to this passage rather than dropping them at a
generic catalogue.

Other careers correctly open nothing. The southern province and the civil diocese
have no compiled geometry. Pointing either at the Trajanic layer would turn the
same word into the same territory. Scandinavia is outside the extent of every
Dacia layer; that absence is the essay's finding, not a missing feature request.

The result is a map that distinguishes three answers: **mapped**, **not compiled**
and **outside coverage**. Most digital maps offer only present or absent. Here the
reason for absence carries the argument, because an unbuilt layer and an irrelevant
extent are not the same epistemic state.

</Section>

<Section id="witness" title="The witness at the release boundary" rooms={['archive', 'border']}>

Historical-map rights do not follow automatically from the age of the work. The
ledger assessed six candidate witnesses. The Hereford Mappa Mundi photograph needs
permission; the Tabula Peutingeriana digitisation, an Ortelius impression and a
coin specimen need object-level rights review; the exact _Notitia_ page remains
unlocated. They stay research-only, so this essay describes them and reproduces
none of their images.

One later document clears the production bar. Decree no. 194 of 16 October 1974
survives on page 1 of the Romanian official bulletin. The scan is sufficient, its
object identity is fixed, and the source page records the official text and its
document photograph as exempt from Romanian copyright protection. The operative
article adds _Napoca_ to _Cluj_. That makes it a useful boundary object for this
essay: rights permit the page to be shown, but the two name-use readings attached
to it remain normalized rather than human-reviewed, so neither is promoted into
the career sequence above.

That asymmetry is deliberate. Reuse permission answers whether the page may be
shown. Scholarly review answers what claims the publication is prepared to make
from it. A permissive licence cannot promote an interpretation, and an excellent
interpretation cannot supply image rights. The two gates meet only at release.

</Section>

<Section id="afterword" title="What a name can and cannot prove" rooms={['border', 'archive', 'map']}>

The wandering in _Nomen Errans_ is not a biography of a word with every missing
year filled. It is a record of distinct, inspectable employments. Between them lie
gaps in evidence, changes of institution, shifts of medium and, in the Scandinavian
case, different ground altogether.

This changes what an atlas can responsibly do. It can compare referents without
merging them. It can direct a reader from a reviewed use to a relevant layer and
back. It can expose the reason a layer is absent. It can record a candidate
relationship without drawing it. What it cannot do is make sameness of spelling
stand in for sameness of territory, people or authority.

The most consequential mark in the diagram is therefore the line that is not
there. A continuous path would be visually satisfying and historically premature.
Leaving it blank makes a smaller claim, but a more useful one: every connection in
an atlas should be able to say who reviewed it, what source supports it and what
kind of relationship it asserts.
